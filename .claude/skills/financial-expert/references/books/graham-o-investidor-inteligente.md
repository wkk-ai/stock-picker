# Graham - O Investidor Inteligente (PT ed., Zweig commentary) - deep notes

### p01
- Front matter + Intro + start of Zweig Intro commentary. Mostly philosophy; few numbers.
- Core definition: investment = thorough analysis promises safety of principal + adequate return; else speculation. Pillars: intrinsic value independent of price; margin of safety (buy below intrinsic value); Mr. Market pendulum.
- Graham-Newman 1936-56: ~14.7%/yr vs 12.2% market (Zweig estimate).
- Defensive investor: avoid serious errors, minimal effort. Enterprising: willing to spend time for above-average selection; Graham doubts extra reward exists in 1972 conditions.
- Bond vs stock yield relation: 1949 bonds 2.66% vs stocks (div yield) 6.82%; end-1964 4.40% vs 2.92%; early 1972 AAA-type bonds 7.19% vs 2.76% industrials. Earlier edition rule: at least 25% in stocks, usual 50/50. Implication for app: always compare earnings/dividend yield to current risk-free/corporate bond yield (for B3: NTN-B/Selic; S&P: 10y UST/Baa).
- Question "how much does it cost?": benchmark = yield on new high-grade utility bonds (9.4% Jun 1970, ~7.3% 1972). Buy stocks like groceries, not perfume. Implication: valuation must be anchored to price, 99% of securities are cheap at some price and dear at another.
- Suggested condition for stock buying: limit to shares priced only slightly above tangible asset value (tangible book = excl. brands, patents, franchises, goodwill, intangibles). Rationale: practical + psychological (less dependence on market moods). Implication: P/TBV (price/tangible book) as a screen/ tie-breaker; flag high P/B growth names as market-mood dependent.
- Obvious physical growth != obvious profit (airlines: revenue boomed, profits negative 1945, 1961, 1970 loss ~US$200M; computers other than IBM mostly unprofitable). Experts cannot reliably pick the winning industries. Implication: do not reward sector growth narrative; weight earnings stability and price paid. Even funds capped IBM ~3% due to price/uncertainty.
- Red flags of 1960s-70s: conglomerates, letter stock, warrants proliferation, misleading names, excess short and long-term debt (Penn Central, Ling-Temco-Vought bankruptcies/overleverage), broker insolvency.
- Behavior: investor's worst enemy is self; temperament beats knowledge; trend-following ("buy because it rose") never worked for 50+ yrs; most funds and broker forecasts underperform; trying slightly more skill can worsen results.
- Dollar-cost averaging in strong stocks defended (Raskob: US$15/mo -> promised US$80k in 20y; actual ~US$8.5k, >8% annual).
- Zweig: a 95% loss needs +1,900% to recover; Newton South Sea; LTCM; 2000-02 US stocks -50.2%; dot-com stocks lost 95%+; claim "old rules obsolete" (Cramer 2000) was wrong. Pendulum swung to excess pessimism by 2003.
- Book note: inflation chapter less relevant for Brazil (editor remark) - but Brazil needs inflation adjustment in earnings/real yields.

### p02
- Ch1 text (Graham). Allocation for defensive investor: bonds 25-75%, stocks inverse; default 50/50, rebalance when drifts ~5%; shift toward 25% stocks if market dangerous, toward 75% if attractive. Implication: market-level valuation (aggregate earnings yield vs bond yield) should modulate how many "cheap" picks to show/how much confidence.
- 1965: bonds ~4.5% taxable, stock div yield ~3.2% (DJIA 892). Normal-level expectation: initial div yield 3.5-4.5% + growth in value roughly equal => ~7.5%/yr combined; 50/50 portfolio ~6%.
- 1971-72: bonds ~7.5-8% gross, DJIA yield 3.5% + ~4% annual appreciation (from retained earnings reinvested) => ~7.5% (Gordon-style: return = dividend yield + earnings growth). Zweig: early 2003 div yield <2% + ~2% growth + ~2% inflation => ~6% expected. Graham: when stock total expected return <= bond yield, bonds clearly preferable. Implication: expected-return model = dividend yield + growth (~ retention x ROE), compared with risk-free.
- Past 20-yr stock return ~14% (post-1949): DJIA price 5x while earnings/dividends ~2x, so most gain came from rising multiples ("self-leveraging"), not intrinsic value -> past returns signal future poor ones. Implication: penalize stocks/sectors whose gains came from multiple expansion; use normalized not trailing returns.
- Long bonds lost up to ~38% in price (20-yr) 1964-70 as rates rose; cash equivalents beat stocks 1964-71. Duration risk exists.
- Defensive stock universe: large, prominent companies with proven profitability and sound finances; utilities included; avoid "hot issues"/IPOs. Index outcomes similar; S&P500 +58% vs DJIA +36% 1960-70, unpredictable relative performance.
- Dollar-cost averaging and formula investing endorsed for defensive.
- Enterprising approaches that DON'T work: market timing/trend following, short-term selectivity (next year's earnings already priced), long-term growth selectivity (prediction errors, priced in). To beat market must follow policies that are sound AND unpopular: buy neglected/unfairly disliked stocks, avoid popular overpriced ones. Contrarian purchases take patience; shorting popular stocks costs money/nerve.
- Special situations (arbitrage, mergers, liquidations, hedges) once gave 20%+/yr, eroded by competition. Graham's 1949 DJIA intrinsic value formula (earnings & interest-rate based) stopped working after 1949; Dow Theory too.
- KEY: net-current-asset stocks (price < current assets minus ALL liabilities, ignoring fixed assets) - "net-nets". 1957 list of ~200; nearly all profitable, outperformed; vanished in 1960s, reappeared at 1970 lows. Implication: net-net screen (market cap < current assets - total liabilities, esp. NCAV x 2/3 later in book) as deep-value flag; works in crashes only.
- Enterprising effort only worth it if expected to add ~5%/yr pre-tax to equity return.
- Speculation rules: keep separate small account; don't add money because market rose; margin trading = speculation; hot IPOs = speculation.
- Zweig fn: Gordon equation = future return = dividend yield + earnings growth. TIPS now inflation hedge; stocks are weak hedge against inflation. 1972-81 stocks 6.5%/yr vs inflation 8.6%. Zweig: shorting loss unlimited. Note 1948 survey: 98% hostile to stocks just before big bull -> sentiment contrarian.

### p03
- Zweig ch1 commentary: speculation traps. Turnover NYSE 20% (1973) -> 105% (2002); funds hold ~11 months. Formula fads decay: January effect (small caps beat by 8.5pp 1962-79, 4.4 pp 80s, 5.8 pp 90-01; but round-trip costs of small caps ~8% (Plexus)); O'Shaughnessy "What Works" (50 stocks: high dividend yield + 5y rising earnings + price/sales <1.5) failed after publication; "Foolish Four" (DJIA 5 lowest price/highest yield) data-mined. Lesson: backtested factor screens are data-mined; require causal link to business performance; published edges erode. Implication for app: keep factors few, economically grounded (earnings power, balance sheet, price paid), avoid over-fit weights and curve-fit thresholds; caveat in methodology.
- Speculation budget: max ~10% of wealth ("mad money"), separate account. Never sell merely because price fell - ask if intrinsic value changed. Test: "would I buy this business on these terms if there were no market quote?"
- Ch2 Inflation (Graham). Inflation history US: 1915-70 avg ~2.5%/yr; 1950-70 ~2.5%; 1965-70 4.5%; 1970 5.4%. He assumed ~3% future (wrong; 1973-82 CPI doubled, ~9%/yr, 13.3% in 1979).
- Stocks vs inflation: no direct short-run link between inflation and stock prices/profits (1966-70: CPI +22%, profits and prices fell). Return on book equity for DJIA ~10% over long run; DJIA 900 vs book 560 in mid-1971 => earnings yield 6.25% on price (P/E 18, P/B ~1.6; "each $1 of book adds ~$1.60 to market price"). Stocks 1915-70: price growth ~4%/yr + ~4% dividend = ~8% total, no better than then-current bond yields.
- Inflation only helps stocks via higher profitability on invested capital; data show it did not (wages > productivity, capital needs rising). Corporate debt ~5x 1950-69 while pretax profits ~2x; interest coverage fell: net profit before tax / debt 30% (1950) -> 13.2% (1969). Part of ROE gains came from leverage at <=4% after-tax cost. Implication: check debt growth vs earnings growth, ROE decomposition (leverage vs margin), interest coverage; high ROE via debt is lower quality.
- Utilities: regulated allowed return on capital, unit prices rose less than CPI so strategic position strong. Implication: utilities (B3 electricity, sanitation) have rate-base pass-through; evaluate with allowed ROE/tariff indexation (IPCA/IGP-M in Brazil).
- Recovery times: GE and DJIA took 25 years to recover from 1929-32. Concentration in stocks fails psychologically; keep balanced; stocks should give more inflation protection than fixed income but no guarantee.
- Gold: $35->$48 in 35y, no yield, storage costs; Zweig counters (gold funds up to 2-5% of assets only). Collectibles, real estate: caution.
- Zweig ch2: money illusion (nominal vs real returns); US inflation >6% in 14 years since 1926: stocks lost in 8, avg return only 2.6%; deflation years terrible too; stocks beat inflation in 50 of 64 five-year periods (78%). 69% of market countries since 1960 had at least one year >=25% inflation (avg 53% purchasing power destroyed). Implication for Brazil app: use real (IPCA-deflated) earnings/returns; high inflation squeezes multiples; compare to real yields (NTN-B). REITs/TIPS (Brazil: FIIs, NTN-B) as inflation hedges.

### p04
- Zweig ch2 end: TIPS (inflation-linked gov bonds) as hedge, ~10%+ of retirement assets; bonds also hedge deflation (Japan since 1989). Implication Brazil: NTN-B (IPCA+) as the real-yield benchmark.
- Ch3 (market history 1871-1972). Market in three eras: 1900-24 ~3%/yr; 1924-49 ~1.5%/yr; 1949-68 boom (Dow 162 -> 995, ~11%/yr ex ~3.5% div). Drawdowns: -44% (1939-42), -36/37% (1968-70); 1961-62 hot issues lost 90%+; IBM 607 -> 300 in 1961-62. 1973-74 -37% (Zweig).
- P/E of S&P composite: 6.3x trailing (Jun 1949) -> 22.9x (Mar 1961); dividend yield 7%+ (1949) -> 3.0% (1961); bond yield 2.60% -> 4.50%. Zweig fn: P/E <10 low, 10-20 moderate, >20 expensive (rule of thumb; Graham cross-ref ch. 7 p.196).
- Method for market level: compare 3-year average earnings P/E (and last year) with earnings yield and dividend yield vs high-grade bond yield (Table 3-3). Jan-1972 verdict: 3-yr avg P/E not high vs history (similar to 1958, lower than 1963/68), BUT earnings yield/dividend yield vs bond yield worst of all dates (div yield half bond yield vs stocks 2x bonds in 1948) -> unattractive for conservatives. Implication: always show earnings yield minus bond yield spread (equity risk premium) alongside P/E; use multi-year average earnings, not single-year.
- Graham's own market-level calls (1948 favorable, 1953 favorable but cautious (too cautious), 1959 dangerous, 1964 too high) were less useful than the steady policy; levels flagged: danger sign absent when IPO/hot-issue carousel is quiet. Implication: IPO/hot-issue frenzy as a market overheating signal.
- 1964 policy at high levels (priority order): no borrowing to buy; no increase in equity share; cut equities to max 50%; do not START dollar-cost averaging at highs.
- 1970 profit rate on capital lowest since WWII; many net losses and bankruptcies.
- Zweig ch3: "Dow 36,000" books extrapolated 7% real since 1802; survivorship bias in early index data (only 7 stocks pre-1840s; 300 firms in 1800 many failed); true historic edge of stocks over bonds overstated ~1-2pp/yr. Implication: backtests of app need delisted/dead companies included (survivorship).
- Return decomposition: real earnings/dividend growth (1.5-2%/yr) + inflation (~2.4%) + dividend yield (1.9%) + speculative change in multiple = ~6% nominal (~4% real). Expectation surveys: investors expect highest returns at peaks (13% -> 18% in 1998-2000; pension plan assumed returns 9.2%).
- Shiller CAPE: price / 10-year average of inflation-adjusted earnings. Above ~20 => poor subsequent market returns; well below 10 => attractive long-run returns. Early 2003 ~22.8; Dec 1999 44.2. Implication: for each stock use 7-10y average real EPS (Graham) rather than single-year EPS; market-level CAPE as context gauge (B3 and S&P).
- "Higher prices = more risk, always." Value of any investment is a function of price paid.
- Ch4 start (defensive investor): return target should depend on intelligent effort, not risk tolerance; undervalued stocks can carry less real risk than a 4.5% bond. Allocation 25-75% each, default 50/50, reduce stock to 25% when market dangerous, raise to 75% when depressed; people naturally do opposite; funds hardly change their equity share.

### p05
- Ch4 (defensive investor), mostly asset allocation and bonds; little stock-picking content.
- Allocation: keep stocks <=50% at 1972 levels; mechanical rebalance (stocks drift to 55% -> sell 1/11 of stock book into bonds; at 45% buy stocks). Optional "temperamental" variant: drop to 25% stocks until DJIA dividend yield reaches 2/3 of the bond yield (e.g. bond 7.5% -> ~5.5%, or DJIA 900 -> ~660). Implication: a simple market-level trigger = stock div/earnings yield as ratio of bond yield (2/3 rule of thumb).
- Yale endowment formula (35% normal equity) abandoned in the 1950s boom; formula plans die when market runs.
- Bond choice: taxable vs tax-free depends on bracket (break-even ~30% tax: 7.5% Aa corp vs 5.3% muni); short vs long maturity = price risk vs yield; savings bonds fine for small investors. Ratings: only Aaa/Aa/A (AAA/AA/A) suffice for defensive; Baa yielded 8.23% vs Aaa 7.19% (1972) - spread ~1pp. Avoid high-yield bonds for defensive (bargains exist but require skill).
- Call provisions are an unfair option against bondholders (AG&E 5% 100-year bond 1928 issued at 101, 62.5 in 1932, called at 106 in 1946 when worth ~160): prefer call protection or discount bonds. Implication: for fixed-income-like equities (preferred, hybrid), consider call/redemption risk.
- Preferred stock: structurally weak (no creditor rights, no upside); only buy when depressed ("buy only when undervalued"); corporate holders get tax advantage. Zweig: if company healthy, why issue preferred not debt? Likely weak; treat as "dead fish". Implication: preferred share (PN/PNA in B3) valuation: dividend priority, check payout of common.
- Income bonds (interest only if earned) are better than preferred; interest is tax-deductible (halves cost of capital) - note on tax shield of debt.
- Zweig commentary: allocation should depend on life circumstances (job risk, spending needs, behavior), NOT age; keep >=25% bonds/cash so you can hold stocks in bear markets; rebalance on a fixed schedule (every 6 months); 100% stocks only if 1yr cash reserve, 20y horizon, held through 2000-02 bear, etc. Intermediate bonds (5-10y) as a default; bond funds beat individual bonds unless Treasuries; avoid annuities with high fees; in 2003, 115 of S&P 500 yielded >=3% -> stocks beating Treasury yield. Never buy stock just for dividend yield; business must be sound and price reasonable.
- Implication: show "dividend yield vs risk-free" but never rank by yield alone; yield trap flag if payout > earnings/FCF.

### p06
- Ch5 KEY: Graham's 4 rules for defensive investor stock selection (central for the app):
  1. Diversify: min 10, max ~30 stocks (watch sector concentration).
  2. Large, prominent, conservatively financed. Concrete: industrials: common equity (at book) >= 50% of total capitalization (incl. bank debt); railroads/utilities: >= 30%. Size (1972): >= US$50M assets or revenue; Zweig modern: market cap >= ~US$10bn (=> ~300 stocks) i.e. x5 inflation scaling for dollars; "prominent" = top 25-33% of its industry.
  3. Long uninterrupted dividend record (since 1950 = 20+ yrs). Zweig modern: at least 10 continuous years (317 of S&P 500) or 20 years (225 of S&P500 end-2002).
  4. Price cap: P/E <= 25x average earnings of last 7 years AND <= 20x last-12-month earnings. (Elsewhere: P/E 15x avg 3-year earnings is the main cap; see later chapters.) This excludes most growth stocks. Implication: compute avg EPS 7y (or 10y real) and trailing EPS; flag P/E7 > 25 or P/E TTM > 20 as expensive for defensive profile.
- Growth stock defined: EPS growth well above market, expected to continue; "real" growth ~doubling EPS in 10 yrs (>7.1% CAGR; rule of 72). Problem is price: growth stocks trade at high multiples of current and past avg earnings -> speculative element. Examples: IBM lost ~50% in 1961-62 and 1969-70; Texas Instruments price 5 -> 256 (EPS 0.40 -> 3.91; price grew 5x faster than EPS), then EPS -50% and price -80% to 49. Zweig: Cisco 2000-02 -76% vs EPS -39%; Microsoft -55.7%. Lesson: price falls much more than earnings when hot stocks de-rate; the hotter the stock, the harder the fall. Implication: growth names get a penalty in a "cheapness" ranking; growth only valued if bought at reasonable multiple.
- Portfolio review once a year; no need for frequent changes if well chosen. Dollar-cost averaging evidence (Tomlinson): across 23 10-year buy periods (1929-52) all profitable at end or within 5 yrs; avg gain 21.5% ex dividends.
- Risk definition (note): true risk = loss of value via actual sale at a bad time, real deterioration of business, or paying too much relative to intrinsic value - NOT mere price fluctuation. A diversified stock group giving satisfactory multi-year returns is "safe". Implication: model risk as permanent-capital-loss risk (leverage, earnings instability, overpayment) rather than volatility/beta only.
- Zweig: "buy what you know" (Lynch) only works with homework (balance sheets, valuation); home bias (company stock in 401k 25-30%; Enron/WorldCom employees); familiarity breeds overconfidence. Dividends reinvested: $1 in 1900 -> $198 (spent dividends) vs $16,797 (reinvested) by 2000; 1929-1954 recovery driven by dividend yield >5.6%/yr. Stocks yield < bonds since 1957, permanent not temporary. Transaction cost one-way 1.25% -> ~0.25% (2000). Index funds with automatic DCA: 3 funds (US stocks 60%, foreign 20%, bonds 20% example). DCA example: $100/mo into S&P from Sept 1929 -> $15,571 in 1939 vs lump sum $12,000 -> $7,223.
- Zweig: trade at most twice a year, 1-2 hours per month for defensive.

### p07
- Ch6 (enterprising, negative): avoid second-grade bonds/preferreds unless bought at deep discount (<~70 or <30% of par for very high-coupon); weak credits distinguished by interest coverage: Graham wants pretax interest covered ~5x for a well-protected railroad bond (CMStP&P covered only 1.5x -> 5% bond yielded 7.35% at 68). Implication: interest coverage (EBIT/interest) thresholds: >=5x pre-tax solid (industrial), 1.5x = junk; use as debt-safety flag in the app.
- "Yield-only" buying is a fallacy ("entrepreneur's investment"): accepting principal-loss risk for +1-2pp income is bad; 1946-47 income bonds fell by 1/3 even while earnings improved. Implication: high dividend yield alone = trap; require coverage and balance-sheet safety.
- Zweig WorldCom case: bond yield 8.3%, pretax income failed to cover fixed charges in 2 of 5 prior years (shortfall ~US$4.1bn), borrowed to pay; bankruptcy 2002; bonds -80%. Flag: fixed-charge coverage <1 in any recent year + rising debt-funded interest + earnings later found overstated by US$7bn.
- Junk bond defaults avg 4.4%/yr since 1978, but 10.5% annual return vs 8.6% Treasuries. EM bond funds <=10% of bond sleeve (Brazil mentioned) - low correlation with US stocks.
- Foreign government bonds: no legal recourse; Czech/Cuba examples. (Relevance: EM sovereign risk for B3.)
- New issues (IPOs, hot offerings) = be skeptical: sold in favorable conditions for the SELLER; underwriters' spread ~7% on IPOs vs <4% on old shares, so more selling push. Companies issue equity near market peaks (Lamont, Schultz). Pattern: bull market -> first IPOs reasonable -> quality deteriorates -> small unseasoned firms priced above established mid-cap names with long records = reliable top signal. New issues often lose 75%+ from offer price (AAA Enterprises $14 -> 28 -> $0.25, 1968-71; Aetna Maintenance $9 -> 15 -> 0.875). IPO counts: 850+ in 1960-62, 781 in 1969, ~4,000 in 1980s, ~5,000 in 1990s, 88 in 2001, 9 in 1974. Implication: the app should exclude/penalize stocks with <5-10 years of listed history, recent IPOs, and show IPO-boom as sentiment gauge; B3 IPOs 2020-21 analog.
- Zweig: average IPO bought at first-day close and held 3 years underperformed market by ~23pp/yr (1980-2001); VA Linux: IPO $30, closed first day $239, US$12.7bn value on revenue $15M/qtr with $10M loss -> $1.19 three years later. "Would you pay this for your neighbor's business?"
- Costs: small-cap round trip 4-8% (Plexus); Barber/Odean: most active traders (>20% monthly turnover) lag market by 6.4pp/yr; patient (0.2%/month) beat market slightly. Day traders need ~10% to break even incl. tax. Implication: turnover discipline; app should not generate frequent buy/sell churn signals.
- Enterprising investor should start with the same 50/50 and only add strategies with well-grounded justification; start-ups of unseasoned firms avoided.

### p08
- Ch7 (enterprising, positive). Four activities: buy in bear/sell in bull (Graham now excludes: no usable formula post-1949; 20 yrs too short sample), selected growth stocks, undervalued stocks, special situations.
- Growth stocks: fund evidence: 45 "growth funds" avg +108% 1961-70 vs S&P +105%, DJIA +83% -> no reward. Zweig: large-growth funds 5.6%/yr, 3.7pp below market over 10y to 2002. Two problems: price already full (P/E >20 = priced for perfection), and growth cannot persist (size makes repetition harder; curve flattens). Defensive cap: 25x 7-yr avg earnings; enterprising: avoid when P/E > ~20. Risk of a stock rises with enthusiasm and with price rising faster than earnings, even if credit rating of firm is top. Big fortunes from one stock come from insiders/controllers; outsiders shouldn't concentrate. Zweig: Intel -22% on only +5% revenue guide vs expected +10% (expectations priced in); use multi-year average earnings, never "next-year earnings" (example: EPS $3 TTM vs $0.50 avg 6y -> 25x TTM = $75 vs 25x 7y avg = $21.43).
- THREE recommended enterprising approaches: (1) Large, unpopular companies (cheap on P/E): buy the 6-10 lowest P/E DJIA stocks each year; Drexel study 1937-69 one-year holds: low-multiple beat DJIA in 25 of 34 years, equal in 6, worse in 3; $10k (1936) -> $66.9k by 1962 (low P/E) vs $44k (all 30) vs $25.3k (high P/E). Failed 1917-33 and failed 1968-71 (sharp loss) -> add other quantitative/qualitative filters. Chrysler caveat: erratic earners show LOW P/E at peak earnings and HIGH P/E at trough (cyclicals) -> use price vs average past earnings to avoid anomalies. (2) Undervalued stocks; (3) special situations.
- Undervalued stock definition: appraised value at least 50% above price (i.e. price <= 2/3 of value). Two valuation tests: (a) earnings power x appropriate multiple; (b) value to a private owner emphasizing realizable assets, esp. net current assets. Sources of undervaluation: disappointing current results (temporary?), unpopularity/neglect, market not recognizing real earnings (accounting masks, e.g. Northern Pacific rail 1946-47 price 36->13.5 with real EPS ~US$10; hidden earnings).
- Filters to avoid value traps (cyclical decline stocks): require reasonable earnings stability over last 10+ years (no loss year), size and financial strength; ideal = big prominent company trading well below its own past average price and average P/E. Anaconda Wire: earnings fell 6 years, price 85 -> 23.5 (value trap). Examples of cheap: National Presto 1970: <4x record EPS and below net current assets; Standard Oil of California 1972: P/E ~10, price ~ book, P/E never above 15 in 13 yrs; GM 1941 (<30 vs EPS >4, div 3.5). Bear markets turn many stocks undervalued.
- Net-current-asset stocks (price < current assets minus ALL liabilities; nothing paid for fixed assets): 1957 list ~150; study of 85 stocks held 2 years: +75% vs +50% for S&P industrials; none lost significantly, 7 flat, 78 gained. Graham: uniformly positive for years; fewer after 1957; 1970 produced new crop; many have small operating profits/losses so diversify. Implication: NCAV screen (market cap < NCAV, ideally <= 2/3 NCAV), diversified basket, with check on cash burn.
- Second-line (non-leading, smaller) companies: market over-penalizes after 1931-32; in 1946 and 1961, 1968 swung to overvaluation (S&P low-priced stocks +280% 1938-46 vs DJIA +40%). Second-line stocks should be bought only when price <= 2/3 of appraised value (undervalued); their central market price sits below fair value. Return sources: high dividend yield, retained earnings, market bounce-ups, periodic re-pricing, new management/policy, takeovers. Rule: defensive investors avoid foreign bonds, ordinary preferred, second-line stocks at full price; enterprising buys them only at <=2/3 of value.
- Special situations/workouts: mergers/acquisitions, reorganizations of bankrupt railroads, utility holding company break-ups (1935 law): market undervalues securities in legal complexity ("never buy a lawsuit"). Needs expertise, declining profitability. Distressed bonds today (Zweig).
- Graham insists on a binary choice: defensive OR enterprising; no half-way ("half-businessman"). Insiders vs outsiders: single share of second-line company worth less than control block; governance/insider issues bigger for small caps. Implication: governance flag (controller treatment of minorities) mostly for non-leading companies (relevant to B3 with controlling shareholders, tag along rights, ON/PN).
- Methodology reminder: Graham demands large samples (often 50 yrs); 22 years too short.

### p09
- Ch7 footnotes/Zweig: small-cap vs large-cap leadership cycles (1975-83 small caps +17.6pp/yr, then -5pp/yr next decade; 1999 small +~9pp). Implication: size premium not stable; do not hard-code small-cap tilt; fashion words in names = hype flag.
- Distressed bonds/restructurings need ~US$100k+ for diversification; "never buy a lawsuit" (Philip Morris, Manville, W.R. Grace, USG). Implication: litigation-overhang flag, exclude from cheap score or penalize.
- Short-term scares (Pfizer/Viagra -3.4%, Warner-Lambert -19%, Carnival -10%) were buying chances: quality company + temporary non-structural shock = opportunity. Implication: detect big 1-day drops in high-quality names with unchanged fundamentals.
- Zweig Ch7: market timing is not feasible; 1966-2001 $1 buy-and-hold $11.71 (Barron's study ignored dividends, real >= $40) vs "miss 5 worst days"=$987 is fantasy. Top 10% timing newsletters 12.6%/yr vs index 16.4% (1991-95). Implication: app should rank by price vs value, not predict market direction.
- Growth stocks: when P/E rises above 25-30 odds become slim. Fortune 500: only 8 of 150 largest grew EPS >=15%/yr over 20 yrs (1960-99); Bernstein: 10% of large US firms grew EPS 20%/yr for 5 consecutive yrs, 3% for 10 yrs, none for 15; study 1951-98: avg EPS growth 9.7%/yr, top-20% largest firms only 9.3%. Implication: cap growth-rate assumptions (fade to ~GDP/9-10%), penalize P/E>25-30 on extrapolated growth; law of large numbers.
- Buy high-quality growth only when temporarily unpopular (J&J -16% day, P/E 24 -> 20; "large relatively unpopular company").
- Nortel (87x est. 2000 earnings, fell 113.50 -> 1.65) and Cisco (121x/98x P/E) managers claimed endless growth; red flag: extreme multiple + management saying growth unlimited.
- Concentration: only 64 of Forbes 400 (1982) remained in 2002 (16%); diversify.
- NCAV screen how-to: current assets minus TOTAL liabilities (incl. preferred and LT debt) vs market cap; find via 52-week-low lists. Example Comverse Oct 2002: CA 2.4bn - liab 1.0bn = NCAV 1.4bn ~ mkt cap <1.4bn (<$8 x <190M sh). Buy a dozen at a time; most such stocks in troubled sectors (telecom, tech). Implication: NCAV screen need balance sheet tags: AtivoCirculante, PassivoTotal, preferred.
- International: Japan 1989 (21.2%/yr vs US 17.5% prior decade) then -2/3 over next decade; home bias risk. Up to 1/3 of equity in foreign/emerging. Implication: cross-market (B3 + S&P) diversification note.
### Ch8 (start; mostly text of Graham)
- Short-maturity (<=7yr) investment-grade bonds negligibly sensitive to price swings; long bonds fluctuate; stocks will fluctuate over any multi-year window.
- Timing (predicting) vs pricing (buy below value, sell above). Minimum for defensive: don't overpay. Timing has no value to investor unless it coincides with pricing (rebuying substantially cheaper than sale price, compensating lost dividends).
- Dow theory and "automatic investment plans" worked until popular, then failed; rule: easily describable, widely followed system stops working. Historic bull-market signs: high price level, high P/E, low dividend yield vs bond yield, heavy margin speculation, many low-quality IPOs. Implication: market-level overvaluation dashboard (P/E, div yield vs bond yield) as context, not trade signal.
- Cycles 1897-1949: 10 full cycles; rises 44%-500% (mostly 50-100%); declines 24%-89% (mostly 40-50%). Expect stock portfolio to rise 50%+ from low and fall 1/3+ from high within five years. DJIA 890 -> 995 (1966) -> 631 (1970) -> 940 (1971).
- Second-line stocks fluctuate more than large ones. 
- Quality paradox: the better the company, the larger the premium over book, the more speculative the price (IBM 607->300 in 7 months 1962-63; 387->219 1970; Xerox 171->87, 116->65).

### Ch11 (security analysis for the layman, Graham)
- Bond/preferred safety tests: interest coverage = earnings (avg of past years, Graham used 7-yr avg and also "weakest year") / interest charges (for preferred: bonds interest + preferred dividends combined). Table 11-1 values not in this part. - Evidence: railroads/utilities/industrials that passed strict coverage tests almost never defaulted; New Haven reorganized 1947 with coverage ~1.1x and failed again in 1961; Penn Central coverage below conservative standards since 1965 (sign to avoid years before 1970 collapse). Industrial interest burden: interest paid by nonfinancial firms $9.8bn (1963) -> $26.1bn (1970); 29% of pre-interest, pre-tax profit vs 16% in 1963. - Common stock analysis: ideal = estimate average future earnings x capitalization factor, compare to price. Group/index forecasts far more reliable than individual company forecasts; hard to tell ex ante which individual forecast is reliable -> diversify. Value Line forecasting example 1964->1968 close on average but many individual misses.
- Capitalization (multiplier) factors: (1) long-term outlook; market multiples often wrong when based on past performance (1963 chemicals at high P/E gave nearly no EPS growth to 1970; oils beat); (2) management: do not count it as separate bullish factor (double counting), useful mainly after recent management change not yet in results (Chrysler 1921, and 1962: EPS $1.24 (1961) -> ~$17 (1963), price 38.5 -> ~200); (3) financial strength/capital structure: cash-rich, no senior claims is better at same EPS; modest debt/preferred fine; high leverage amplifies upside; (4) dividend record: uninterrupted dividends 20+ years = important quality test (defensive investor may restrict to these); (5) current payout: standard ~2/3 of average earnings (1969: Dow 59.9%, all US 55%); when payout normal, valuing by earnings or dividends gives same result (EPS 3, div 2: 12x earnings = 18x dividend = 36). Low-payout growth cos. discussed in Ch19.
- GRAHAM GROWTH FORMULA (Ch11): Value = normal (current) EPS x (8.5 + 2g), g = expected annual growth rate over next 7-10 years (in % points). Reverse it to get growth implied by price (Table 11-5: Xerox implied 32.4%, GM 2.8%). Implication: implement as intrinsic value estimate; cap g (see Zweig Ch7 growth persistence); compare to price; ensure normalized EPS (multi-year average). With zero growth, multiple = 8.5 (Graham's no-growth P/E).
- Paradox: formal math valuation is used most exactly where projections are least reliable (high-multiple growth stocks): the more value depends on future vs demonstrated past, the larger the error risk.

### Ch12 (EPS pitfalls, Graham)
- Do not take one year's EPS seriously; read the footnotes. Alcoa 1970 example: headline "primary EPS" 6.32 annualized from Q4 (P/E <10) vs net after special charges ~2.80 annualized (P/E ~22); four different EPS figures; dilution from convertibles (5.20 -> 5.01 fully diluted). Implication: use fully diluted EPS and multi-year normalized EPS; never annualize one quarter.
- Special charges pitfalls: reserves for future closures taken in a bad year (1970 "big bath") so later years look better, charges never hit EPS, tax-credit loss carryforwards inflate later net income; real operations always have some losers, so "extraordinary" costs recur. Other distortions: depreciation method (Trane 1970 EPS +20% half from switch to straight-line), R&D capitalization vs expensing, FIFO vs LIFO, dilution from convertibles/warrants (can cut EPS by half). Rule: ignore items only if small; correct EPS = diluted EPS minus portion of special charges attributable to the year (best: spread over <=5 years). Implication: app EPS normalization: average last 5-10 years of diluted EPS including write-offs; flag companies with frequent "non-recurring" items.

### Ch14 (stock selection for the defensive investor) - CORE CRITERIA
- Two routes: DJIA-type diversified portfolio (today index fund) or quantitatively tested portfolio. Graham's SEVEN criteria (arbitrary, for defensive investors):
  1. Size: annual sales >= US$100M (industrial) or total assets >= US$50M (utility) in 1971 dollars. Zweig update: market cap >= ~US$2bn (2003); 437 of S&P 500 pass.
  2. Financial strength: industrials current ratio >= 2.0; long-term debt <= net current assets (working capital); utilities: debt <= 2x equity (book). (Zweig: LT debt includes preferred, excludes deferred taxes; ~120 S&P 500 firms had current ratio >= 2 in early 2003.)
  3. Earnings stability: positive earnings in each of the last 10 years.
  4. Dividend record: uninterrupted payments for at least 20 years.
  5. Earnings growth: EPS up at least 1/3 over last 10 years using 3-yr averages at start and end (~3.4%/yr).
  6. Moderate P/E: price <= 15x average earnings of last 3 years.
  7. Moderate P/B: price <= 1.5x latest book value; but rule of thumb: P/E x P/B <= 22.5 (e.g., 9x earnings with 2.5x book acceptable).
- Rule: portfolio's earnings yield (inverse of P/E) should be at least equal to high-grade (AA) bond yield; 7.5% AA -> P/E <= 13.3. Zweig fn3: early 2003 AA 4.6% -> max P/E 21.7; average stock ~20% below the max -> ~17x 3-yr avg earnings; >200 (40%) of S&P 500 had P/E (3y avg) <= 17. Implication for app (B3/S&P): make the P/E ceiling a function of AA/NTN-B/Selic yield; P/E x P/B <= 22.5 and also earnings yield vs. bond yield comparison; use 3-yr avg earnings (real/IPCA adjusted for B3).
- DJIA at end-1970: all criteria met in aggregate narrowly (P/E 15 on 3yr avg, P/B 1.5, 10-yr 3yr-avg growth +77%); only 5 of 30 passed all individually (American Can, AT&T, Anaconda, Swift, Woolworth); Graham guessed ~100 stocks in S&P Guide passed. Financial strength was the toughest test (many large firms had weakened current ratios and heavy debt).
- Utilities: exempt from current ratio; require equity-to-debt balance; most utilities fit defensive criteria, trade near book, higher dividend yield, more stable (but Zweig: utilities far more volatile now due to nuclear write-offs, California deregulation; hold via diversified fund). Sector P/E relative switched between industrials and utilities over two decades; rotate if growth got excessive; selling and paying tax better than holding and regretting.
- Financials (banks, insurers, S&L): apply same price-to-earnings and price-to-book arithmetic; financial soundness matters more because short-term liabilities >> equity; sector indexes move inversely at times (NY banks index 44.3 vs life insurers 218 in 1970 on 1941-43=10). Implication: banks/insurers need own treatment (no current ratio; use P/B, ROE, capital ratios).
- Railroads (and airlines today): no compelling reason to own; Penn Central (paid dividends 120+ yrs) went bankrupt 1970 after trading near highs in 1968-69. Only buy if value so large it would be irrational to look elsewhere. Implication: weak-industry flag for airlines/capital-intensive transport.
- Selectivity: 100 analysts would pick very different "best 5 of Dow"; price already reflects known facts and expectations (EMH remark in fn9). Two approaches: forecasting (qualitative: prospects, management) vs protection (quantitative: margin of safety between price and indicated value). Graham committed to quantitative/protective approach; "do not accept future promises as compensation for lack of present value." Defensive investor: emphasize diversification over selection.
- Zweig commentary Ch14: use total-market index fund as core; if picking stocks, keep ~90% index / 10% own picks. Diversification protects vs MicroStrategy (-99.5%, accounting fraud) and captures rare "superperformers" (30 best stocks 1972-2002 included Southwest Airlines, Worthington, Dollar General, UST - unpredictable). Post-2000 note: former hot growth stocks became net-cash, high current-ratio firms in 2003 (Fig 14-1) - Graham's strength tests still find bargains in the rubble of a bear market.

### Ch15 (stock selection for the enterprising investor, Graham)
- Funds (with research staff, expenses ~0.5%/yr) failed to beat S&P 500 in long periods; 1960-68 random equal-weight NYSE portfolios beat mutual funds in same risk class by 3.7%/yr (low risk), 2.5% (medium), 0.2% (high). Selection beating market is hard; approach only via segments ignored by analysts. Analysts' flaw: buy best-growth industries at any price; real firms rarely grow forever nor vanish -> mean reversion, fluctuations "poverty to riches and back".
- Graham-Newman methods (1926-56): arbitrage and liquidations (need >=20% annualized and >=80% probability of success), related hedges (convertible vs short common), NET CURRENT ASSET (net-net) stocks: buy below net current asset value ALONE (no value to fixed assets), typically at <= 2/3 of NCAV, diversified in >=100 issues most years. Dropped: "attractive" stocks not below working capital, and unrelated hedges.
- Enterprising investor: avoid overvalued by conservative standards, be less rigid (strength in one category may offset weakness in another; e.g. do not drop a company with a 1970 loss if average earnings are high). Buy cyclicals when out of favor/short-term outlook poor. Do not pay high price for enthusiasm.
- ENTERPRISING SCREEN (secondary companies, S&P Stock Guide, end 1970): P/E (last 12m) <= 9; plus: (1) current assets >= 1.5x current liabilities and debt <= 110% of net current assets (industrials); (2) no loss in last 5 yrs; (3) some current dividend; (4) last-year EPS > EPS of 1966 (5 yrs earlier); (5) price < 120% of tangible book (net tangible assets). No minimum size. ~1 in 10 stocks has P/E<=9; 20 low-P/E stocks -> 5 passed; 450 stocks -> 15 passed (~150 of 4,500). Optional 7th filter: S&P earnings/dividend rating B+ (average) or higher -> ~100 stocks remain.

### Ch16 (convertibles and warrants, Graham)
- Convertibles: investor gives up quality or yield; issues come late in bull markets -> poor results; convertible preferreds fell more than commons Dec1967-Dec1970; of 20 sampled preferreds only 1 rose, 14 fell badly. ### Ch19 (shareholders and managers; dividend policy, Graham)
- Shareholders have right to question mgmt if results (1) poor in absolute terms, (2) worse than peers, (3) poor market price over long period. Bad managements typically changed via takeovers not shareholder action.
- Dividend policy: historically 60-75% payout; Graham: shareholders should demand normal payout (~2/3 of earnings) unless mgmt shows reinvested earnings produce satisfactory growth in EPS. Low payout by weak firms "to expand" is illogical (no a priori reason mediocre business improves owners' wealth with retained cash). Weak-balance-sheet firms may legitimately retain earnings to repay debt. Market treats fast growers by growth, "income" stocks by yield; many companies in between -> hard.

### Ch20 (margin of safety, Graham) - CORE MARGIN OF SAFETY RULES
- Bonds/preferreds: margin = earnings covering fixed charges (railroad: >=5x pre-tax over several years for investment grade); alternative = enterprise value vs debt (business worth $30M vs $10M debt). Margin removes need for precise forecast; test covers below-normal business years.
- Common stocks: margin = expected earning power well above bond yield. Example: earnings power 9% of price vs bond rate 4% -> annual 5% margin; over 10 yrs excess ~50% of price paid (retained earnings roughly added to value). 1972: normal stock at 12x earnings (8.33% yield) with 4% dividend gives too thin margin vs bond rates -> real risk; but can't avoid stocks due to inflation. In 1932-33 many strong industrials sold below value of bonds they could issue -> ideal; National Presto 1972: market cap $43M vs pre-tax earnings $16M (could support equivalent debt).
- Biggest losses come from buying low-quality securities in good times (good current earnings mistaken for earning power; IPOs at huge premium to tangible assets). Coverage must be tested over several years including a below-normal year (1970-71).
- Growth stocks: margin comes from projected earnings; dangerous because market prices them above conservative projection; forecasts differing from past should be underestimated slightly; margin always depends on price paid ("large at one price, small at higher, nonexistent at higher still").
- Undervalued securities: margin = gap between price and appraised value; buy second-line stocks at <= 2/3 of indicated value ("two thirds or less") — core enterprising rule; need diversification; if prospects definitely bad, avoid at any price.
- Diversification and margin: with positive margin, more positions -> higher certainty of net profit (insurance logic); with negative margin (roulette) diversification worsens.
- Investment vs speculation: margin of safety must be demonstrable by numbers, reasoning, experience, not subjective. "Unconventional" investments for enterprising investors: undervalued second-line stocks at <=2/3 of value; discounted medium-grade bonds/preferreds (1930s real-estate bonds at 10c with value 4-5x).

## Book synthesis

### (a) Numeric criteria and rules (Graham, chapter refs; Zweig updates marked Z)

**Defensive investor - Ch14 (seven criteria)**
1. Size: industrial sales >= US$100M; utility total assets >= US$50M (1971 dollars). Ch5 gave >= US$50M assets/revenue and "prominent" (top 25-33% of industry). Z: market cap ~US$2bn (Ch14, 2003) or ~US$10bn (Ch5). Scale by inflation/currency for B3.
2. Financial strength: industrial current ratio >= 2.0 AND long-term debt <= net current assets (working capital). Utilities: debt <= 2x book equity. Ch5: common equity >= 50% of total capitalization (industrial), >= 30% (rail/utility). Z: LT debt includes preferred, excludes deferred taxes; Ch14-box: LTD < 50% of total capital.
3. Earnings stability: positive EPS in each of last 10 years (Z: 86% of S&P 500 passed 1993-2002).
4. Dividend record: uninterrupted for >= 20 years (Z: ok to use >= 10 yrs; 255 of S&P 500 had 20+ yrs).
5. Earnings growth: EPS up >= 1/3 over 10 years comparing 3-yr averages at start and end (~3.4%/yr). Z: raise to +50% (~4%/yr) or +100% (~7%/yr).
6. P/E: price <= 15x AVERAGE EPS of last 3 years (Ch5 alternative: <= 25x 7-yr average and <= 20x last-12-month EPS). Never forward P/E.
7. P/B: price <= 1.5x latest book; combined rule P/E x P/B <= 22.5 (e.g. 15 x 1.5; 9x with 2.5x book ok). Ch8 stricter variant: price <= ~1.33x TANGIBLE book. Ch1: only buy "slightly above tangible asset value".
- Yield tie: portfolio earnings yield >= AA/high-grade bond yield (7.5% AA -> P/E <= 13.3; Z 2003: AA 4.6% -> 21.7, average pick ~17x 3-yr avg).
- Diversification: 10-30 stocks (Ch5). Avoid IPOs/hot issues. Z: 60%+ institutional ownership = overowned; read 5 yrs of filings.
- Bond/stock context: dividend yield of market vs bond yield; stocks 25%-75% of portfolio, default 50/50; reduce to 25% until DJIA dividend yield >= 2/3 of bond yield.

**Enterprising investor - Ch15 (relaxed screen on second-line companies)**
1. P/E (last 12 months) <= 9 ("multiple of nine or less") as first filter (about 1 in 10 stocks).
2. Current assets >= 1.5x current liabilities AND debt <= 110% of net current assets (industrials).
3. No loss in last 5 years.
4. Some current dividend.
5. Last-year EPS > EPS five years earlier (Graham: 1970 > 1966).
6. Price < 120% of net tangible assets (tangible book).
- Optional 7th: S&P earnings/dividend quality rating B+ or better (cuts to ~100 stocks of 4,500). No minimum size; buy as a diversified group. Be less rigid: a strength can offset a weakness (e.g. one loss year if average earnings high).
- Ch7 enterprising: undervalued = appraised value >= 50% above price (price <= 2/3 of value); defensive growth cap 25x 7-yr avg; enterprising avoid P/E > ~20 growth; low-P/E large-company approach: buy the 6-10 lowest P/E Dow stocks (beat DJIA 25 of 34 years); use average not peak/trough EPS for cyclicals (Chrysler caveat).
- Ch15 style: Hawkins "60-cent dollars" (price <= 60% of appraised value). Experiments: random 30-stock lists lost 22%; A+ S&P quality industrials +9.5%; combination of criteria beats single-factor.

**Net-net / NCAV rules**
- NCAV = current assets - TOTAL liabilities (including preferred and long-term debt, contingencies); fixed assets, goodwill, intangibles valued at zero. (Not simply working capital; Zweig fn10 Ch15.)
- Buy when price < NCAV; Graham-Newman typically bought at <= 2/3 of NCAV; diversify into >= ~100 issues most years (Zweig: a dozen at a time; Ch7 study 85 stocks +75% in 2 yrs vs S&P industrials +50%, 78 gained, 7 flat, none lost significantly).
- Exclude: those reporting net loss over last 12 months (Ch15 1971 list); check cash burn; many are small with weak operating profits. Appears mainly after crashes (1932, 1957, 1970, 2002). Works over 1923-57 except 1930-32. Needs patience (Burton-Dixie +165% in 3.5 yrs). Examples: National Presto 1970 (price < NCAV, <4x EPS), A&P 1938 (market cap < cash).
- Stricter variant (Ch20): market cap < debt capacity of business (National Presto 1972: $43M cap vs $16M pretax).

**Margin of safety rules (Ch20 plus Ch1/Ch8)**
- Bonds: pretax coverage of fixed charges >= 5x for railroads/utility-like (Penn Central 1.9x = fail; Ch11: coverage tested on 7-yr average and weakest year; alt: pretax earnings / total debt >= 33% industrial, 20% utility, 25% rail); business value >> debt.
- Stocks: expected earnings power (1/PE using normalized EPS) well above bond yield; margin = earnings yield - bond yield (9% vs 4% = 5%/yr; over 10 yrs ~50% of price). 1972: earnings yield ~= bond yield = zero margin; 1965 P/E 11/yield 9%.
- Undervalued: price <= 2/3 of appraised value (>= 50% upside); "60-cent dollar"; Buffett: bridge 15,000 kg vs 5,000 kg trucks; estimate roughly, no need for last cent.
- Growth: use conservative projections, underestimate; margin depends on price paid.
- Valuation formula (Ch11): V = normalized EPS x (8.5 + 2g), g = expected 7-10 yr growth %; reverse to see implied g; cap g (about 5-7%); with g >= 8% the formula explodes.
- Average earnings: 7-10 year arithmetic average of EPS (includes special items) for P/E; growth = last 3-yr avg vs previous period avg; stability = max EPS decline in 10 yrs vs prior 3-yr avg (0% best).
- Diversification only helps if the margin is positive (roulette logic).
- Purchase price rules: never pay P/E > ~25 (7-yr), >20 (TTM) in defensive; P/E above 25-30 gives slim odds (Ch7 Z).

### (b) Top 10 implications for the app

1. Core score = "price paid vs normalized earnings power": use 3-yr average EPS (defensive P/E <= 15) AND 7-10 yr average real/diluted EPS (CAPE-style), never forward estimates; show earnings yield minus risk-free (NTN-B/Selic for B3, 10y UST/AA for S&P) as the margin-of-safety readout, and let the P/E ceiling float with bond yields (max P/E = 1/AA yield).
2. Implement Graham's seven defensive tests as a transparent pass/fail checklist with adaptable thresholds: size (scaled), current ratio >= 2, LTD <= NCAV (or LTD/capital < 50%), no loss year in 10 yrs, dividend years (use available history, 10+ if needed), EPS +33% to +50% over 10 yrs on 3-yr averages (IPCA-adjusted for B3), P/E <= 15 (3-yr avg), P/B <= 1.5, P/E x P/B <= 22.5. Show count of tests passed; use the Graham Number-style product as the ranking filter.
3. Add an "enterprising/second-line" mode: P/E (TTM) <= 9, CA/CL >= 1.5, debt <= 110% NCAV, no 5-yr loss, some dividend, EPS above 5 yrs ago, price <= 120% tangible book; no min size but require liquidity; flag value traps by requiring 10-yr stability and ROE not chronically low.
4. Net-net module: NCAV = current assets - total liabilities (incl. preferred, debt); flag price < NCAV and price <= 2/3 NCAV; exclude loss-making/cash-burning; expect few in normal markets (show count as market-cheapness gauge); recommend diversification (10+ names) in the UI.
5. Value-trap / quality filters: low ROE (Harvester 5.5%, Alcoa) means cheap-to-book is not enough; require ROE/ROIC (Davis owner-earnings ROIC >= 10%, 6-7% acceptable only with strong brand), margin stability, earnings stability; compare P/E to ROE jointly (value ~ EPS^2/BV); beware cyclicals' inverted P/E (low at peak, high at trough) using normalized EPS.
6. Balance-sheet and debt-service flags: interest coverage EBIT/interest (>= 5x pre-tax solid, <1.5-2 junk; check weakest year), debt growth vs earnings growth, LTD/total capital > 50%, current ratio < 1.5-2, equity/capitalization < 50% (industrial)/30% (utility); zero cash taxes while reporting profit; negative operating cash flow funded by financing (Penn Central, LTV, WorldCom, Exodus).
7. Accounting-quality and dilution checks: use fully diluted EPS (add warrants/convertibles; options > 3% of shares flagged); recurring "non-recurring" charges, capitalized operating costs, pension income > 5% of NI or assumed return > 6.5%, aggressive revenue recognition, receivables/inventory growing faster than sales (Nortel), customer financing, goodwill/intangibles large vs equity (deduct for tangible book), big acquisitions of loss-making targets, serial acquirers (> 2-3/yr), "deferred" assets exceeding equity (NVF). Presentation and behavior design: sweet spot is the unglamorous middle (solid, average-growth, mid-quality, low multiple); display multi-year backtests with survivorship-free data (value underperforms ~40% of years), state that factor edges decay and thresholds are not data-mined; no price-momentum alerts; market-level context panel (aggregate P/E on 3-yr/10-yr EPS, earnings yield vs bond yield, 2/3 dividend-yield rule); require diversification (10-30 names, sector caps) and "would I buy this business without a quote?" prompt; tax/costs and break-even gain displayed.
