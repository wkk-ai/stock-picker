# McKinsey Valuation (Koller, Goedhart, Wessels, 4th ed.) - deep notes

### p01
- Front matter + Ch1 start (Why Maximize Value). Core thesis: value = invest capital at ROIC > cost of capital; growth creates value only when ROIC > WACC.
- Long-run US equity real return ~6.5%/yr (~200 yrs); decomposition: real profit growth 3-3.5% + cash yield (dividends+buybacks) 3-3.5%.
- Real median P/E mean-reverts to ~15 (normal level); payout ~50% for typical firm with ROE 12%, real growth 3.5%. Cash yield = (1/PE) x payout.
- Payout ratio driven by growth and ROIC (value driver link: payout = 1 - g/ROE).
- Market vs intrinsic: DCF-predicted P/E tracked actual median P/E 1962-2003 except late-1990s bubble. Deviations short-lived and sector-concentrated.
- S&P 1980-99 rise decomposed: earnings growth (+302 pts at constant PE, 6.9% nominal/3.2% real), falling inflation/rates (10y yield 15% in 1981 to 5.7% in 1999 lifted P/Es), and megacap/TMT spread (+376 pts). During high inflation P/Es were very low because ROIC could not rise with cost of capital.
- Implication for app: P/E must be judged relative to rates/inflation regime (B3 with high Selic: lower fair P/E; compare earnings yield vs NTN-B/Treasury). Cheap P/E in high-inflation regime is not necessarily cheap.
- Bubbles concentrated in sectors (TMT, megacaps): median S&P stock fell only 8% in 2000-03 bear while cap-weighted index fell ~40%; 40% of stocks rose. Implication: use sector-relative and median-based comparisons, not index-cap-weighted ones.
- Index-level P/E averages are distorted by megacaps (megacap P/E was 2x the other 470 in 1999): use median not average for universe benchmarks.
- Country 100-yr real returns 2.5% (Belgium) to 7.6% (Sweden); countries with inflation/upheaval lowest. Higher US P/E and market-to-book vs Europe/Asia explained mostly by higher ROIC/ROE (market-to-book tracks ROE) -> implication: P/B should be read together with ROE; cross-country (B3 vs S&P) multiple gaps partly justified by ROIC and growth.
- Red flags from LBO bubble: projections assuming margins/growth/capital turnover never achieved in the industry; plan relies on reselling at higher price; disciplines decline as participants multiply. Behavioral: extrapolating recent returns ("Dow 36,000") and "new era" thinking.

### p02
- Rest of Ch1 + most of Ch2 (EG Corporation case; narrative, but with useful heuristics).
- Internet bubble red flags: "increasing returns/network effects" misapplied to businesses with easy switching (mobile carriers) or physical scaling costs (online grocery); only a few cases (e.g. Office, 75% margins) justify permanently high returns. Competition drives ROIC back to reasonable levels in most industries -> app should not extrapolate high ROIC/margins indefinitely.
- Analysts that cannot justify value resort to relative valuation only (herding) -> relative multiples alone can be circular; anchor with absolute/DCF-style cross-check.
- Short-termism: 55% of 401 execs would delay/cancel a value-creating project to hit quarterly EPS consensus. Accounting fraud: fraudulent profits must eventually become real. Implication: flag earnings quality (cash conversion, accruals) and companies that manage to EPS.
- Evidence: top-TRS firms also had higher employment growth and R&D growth (value focus != short-sighted).
- EG case heuristics: market-value-to-invested-capital (EV/IC) compare vs peers (EG 1.9x vs peers 1.8-4.0x); acquisitions at premium + EPS-accretion-neutral financing destroyed value; stock fell by ~ the premium paid. Flag serial acquirers whose acquisitions lack synergies/ROIC.
- Value-driver sensitivity: for a business with ROIC < WACC (Foodco ROIC 9%), faster growth DESTROYS value; growth is good only when ROIC > WACC. Consumerco ROIC 30%+ but growth only ~inflation -> value in growth/pricing. Implication: score = ROIC-WACC spread and growth jointly, not growth alone.
- Cash-flow map: a firm paying dividends with borrowing while reinvesting cash flows into low-return units = red flag (dividends funded by debt; capital misallocation).
- Repeated downward EPS revisions (analyst estimate misses) = credibility/red-flag signal.
- Conglomerate/holding: sum-of-the-parts using peer EV/EBITA, market-to-book; corporate overhead capitalized was a ~25% value drag (-1,275 of 6,450). Breakup gains only if parts worth more than market after taxes; liquidation value only makes sense for property-rich or negative-spread financial businesses (consumer finance whose new loans lose value).
- Scenario valuation of units: strategic buyer > LBO > spin-off > liquidation in the case; value to strategic buyer can be ~1.5x as-is value -> takeover risk/undervaluation signal for strong-cash-flow, dominant-brand, under-managed firms.
- Capital structure: moving AA to BBB, +$1.5bn debt, worth ~$600m PV tax shield (about 8% of equity value); firms with stable cash flows can carry more debt; securitization/leasing deals rarely create value for investment-grade firms.
- ROIC definition (Ch2): after-tax operating profit / (working capital + fixed assets). Value drivers = long-term revenue growth + ROIC vs cost of capital; set targets by business type.
- Hurdle rates should differ by division (opportunity cost of capital by risk); acquisitions: cash-flow value to buyer must exceed price paid; pay only for own-standalone value + improvements, not all synergies.

### p03
- Ch2 end + Ch3 (Fundamental Principles) + start of Ch4. KEY FORMULA CHAPTER.
- ROIC = NOPLAT / Invested Capital (IC = operating working capital + PP&E etc.). NOPLAT = EBIT less adjusted operating taxes. Net investment = IC(t+1)-IC(t). FCF = NOPLAT - net investment = NOPLAT x (1 - IR). IR = net investment/NOPLAT. g = RONIC x IR (so IR = g/RONIC).
- Key value driver ("Zen") formula: Value(EV) = NOPLAT(t+1) x (1 - g/RONIC) / (WACC - g). Valid for constant g and ROIC; the book does not use it in practice (too restrictive) but uses it to frame what drives value.
- Economic profit EP = IC x (ROIC - WACC) = NOPLAT - IC x WACC. Value = IC + PV(EP) -> same as DCF. Growing perpetuity: PV(EP) = EP1/(WACC-g). Example: IC 500, ROIC 20%, WACC 10%, EP 50, g 5% -> PV(EP) 1000 -> value 1500.
- Rule: maximize economic profit (spread x capital), not ROIC. Closing a store earning above WACC lifts average ROIC but cuts value (Fred: 18% ROIC, WACC 10%, EP 800k). Implication: rank by ROIC-WACC spread AND scale, do not reward high ROIC from shrinking capital base.
- Growth rule: if RONIC > WACC, more growth adds value; = WACC, growth neutral; < WACC, growth destroys value. High-ROIC firms create more value by growing; low-ROIC firms by raising ROIC. Table (NOPLAT 100, WACC 10%, 25y fade): at ROIC 7.5% value falls as g rises (887/708/410 for g 3/6/9%); at ROIC 10% value 1000 regardless; at ROIC 20% 1170/1442/1886.
- Identical earnings and earnings growth can imply different value: Value Inc (ROIC 20%, reinvest 25%) worth 1500 (15x earnings) vs Volume Inc (ROIC 10%, reinvest 50%) worth 1000 (10x). Core point: EARNINGS MULTIPLES ARE NOT COMPARABLE without ROIC; cash flow after reinvestment is what counts. Implication for app: pair P/E with ROIC/ROE and reinvestment rate; a low P/E on a low-ROIC capital-hungry business is not cheap.
- Multiples: EV/NOPLAT = (1 - g/RONIC)/(WACC - g). EV/IC = ROIC x (1 - g/RONIC)/(WACC - g) -> P/B (EV/IC) is driven by ROIC and growth; at ROIC=WACC EV/IC = 1. Growth stocks are not only about growth; ROIC matters equally. Example: P&G and Lowe's both ~20x earnings: P&G implied ROIC 38%/growth 5%; Lowe's ROIC 12%/growth 9%. Method: solve backward from the multiple for market-implied growth given ROIC (reverse DCF).
- Multiples useful as sanity check and shorthand; industry-average multiples dangerous because of accounting differences, inflation, cyclicality, differing ROIC/growth; a good multiple analysis needs as much work as a forecast.
- Value of a company = PV of future performance, not past or asset cost; investor return depends on performance versus EXPECTATIONS (point-spread analogy). Fred: intrinsic $20/share vs $7 invested capital; if expectations met, return = cost of capital (10%). A stock expecting 25% ROIC that delivers 20% falls though ROIC > WACC. Behavioral: priced-for-perfection stocks are fragile; low expectations + good fundamentals = opportunity.
- Cash flow in one year is a poor performance measure (can be manipulated by delaying capex/advertising/R&D; big negative FCF fine if investing at ROIC > WACC). Heineken FCF history jumped around (e.g., -1,124 to +648 projected); focus on ROIC/growth drivers, not raw FCF. Implication: use normalized/multi-year FCF.
- Heineken example numbers: revenue growth 10.7% (1999-2003) -> 7.2% proj; ROIC (after goodwill) 13.9% -> 8.9%; WACC 8.2% -> 7.5%. Note ROIC "after goodwill" (includes acquisition premium) – acquisitive firms show lower ROIC incl. goodwill.
- Book value of IC does not change value (overstated IC lowers PV of EP by same amount) - but affects EP/ROIC reading: accounting differences distort ROIC and P/B comparisons.
- ROIC and accounting measures can be manipulated short term; need health indicators (pricing, share, unit cost, quality, customer satisfaction). Does ROIC come from higher prices with falling share or stable price growing share? Look beyond number.
- Compensation/behavior: bonuses tied to short-term accounting profit; EPS boosted by buybacks is lower quality than EPS from revenue/share growth (investors care about drivers of earnings).
- Ch4 begins: question of whether fundamentals drive markets (continues next part).

### p04
- Ch4 (Do Fundamentals Drive the Stock Market?) first ~2/3. Empirical support for ROIC+growth valuation.
- Valuation LEVEL (MV/IC) driven by long-term ROIC and growth; TRS (returns) driven by CHANGES IN EXPECTATIONS, not absolute performance. Implication: a cheap-by-fundamentals stock only pays if expectations improve/ are met; high-quality companies already priced for it give no excess return.
- Regression on 563 N. American firms (10y sales growth and 10y avg ROIC vs MV/IC incl goodwill, 2003): ROIC+growth explain 46% of MV/IC variation. Slopes: ROIC 19.3 (t 21.5), growth 2.0 (t 3.4). Within ROIC cohorts growth matters only when ROIC high: growth slope insignificant for ROIC 0-6% (0.25), 6-9% (0.76), 12-15% (2.14, p16%); significant for 9-12% (3.22) and >15% (7.99). Market does not reward growth that earns below cost of capital. Implication: score growth conditional on ROIC>WACC; discount growth for low-ROIC firms.
- Commodity chemicals (130 firms, 1963-2001) MV/capital: high ROIC+high growth 1.6; high ROIC low growth 1.5; low ROIC high growth 0.5 (!); low ROIC low growth 1.3 -> growth with sub-WACC returns is penalized heavily. Industry avg ROIC ~ cost of capital in chemicals.
- Theoretical grid (WACC 8%, 10y competitive advantage period then ROIC=WACC): higher ROIC => higher MV/IC; growth adds only if ROIC > WACC.
- DCF using long-term history + IBES consensus matched market MV/capital closely (index 95-108) for 5 big names each in electric utilities, consumer goods, pharma; oil majors and some pharma 74-99. So DCF with ROIC/growth works across sectors.
- Intel example: net income nearly doubled but stock -11% because sales/margin below expectations. Over 15+ years TRS tracks earnings; short term tracks expectation changes. Regression S&P500 1993-2003: change in expectations (analyst EPS revisions, LTG) explains ~18% of TRS variance; absolute cash flow/EP explain ~0-2%.
- Market is long-term: PV of 5 years of expected dividends averages only 8.7% of share price for S&P 500 sample (range 1.4% Texas Inst to 16% Dow; IBM 3.3%, Wal-Mart 3.4%; assumes 7% div growth, rf 4.3%, MRP 5%). Implication: most of value sits in terminal/continuing value; sensitivity to long-term assumptions is large.
- EPS misses matter only if they change long-term outlook: 137 announcements; lower EPS surprise + negative change in long-term expectations = -4.1% abnormal; lower surprise with positive LT outlook +2.3%. Earnings surprises explain <2% of price volatility; >40% of firms with positive surprise had negative return. Pharma: pipeline events move price 8-25%.
- Market rewards R&D, advertising, capex when growth opportunities are value-creating; negative reaction when outlook weak. Write-offs (non-cash restructuring) viewed positively. Implication: do not penalize high R&D/capex firms by earnings alone; check ROIC on new investment.
- Accounting changes irrelevant to price when cash flows unchanged: US GAAP reconciliation (earnings differing >30% in half of cases) had no negative effect; goodwill amortization abolition: price effect gone within 2 weeks, unrelated to size; goodwill impairments: stocks had already fallen ~35% in prior 6 months (Time Warner -37% before $54bn writedown). Implication: use cash-based/economic metrics (EBITDA/EBITA, FCF, ROIC ex-goodwill and incl goodwill) rather than reported EPS; impairments are lagging signals; stock option expensing no price effect (value already in price if disclosed) -> adjust for SBC in own metrics.
- LIFO vs FIFO: in rising prices LIFO lowers reported profit but raises after-tax cash flow -> price rises on LIFO adoption. Cash flow over profit. (Relevance: inflationary markets like Brazil - inventory accounting and inflation distort earnings.)
- Behavioral finance conditions for persistent mispricing: irrational investors + systematic patterns + limits to arbitrage (shorting costs, noise-trader risk). Patterns: long-term reversal (past winners become losers; IPO/SEO underperformance, issuers issue after strong performance) and short-term momentum (months). Fama-French: reversals may reflect risk premia tied to market-to-book and size (distress/liquidity). Momentum profits small after costs. Expected abnormal return across studies ~0.
- Mispricing cases: Palm/3Com carve-out (parent worth negative), Royal Dutch/Shell twin shares up to 30% gap, Unilever NV/PLC; resolved when convergence date set. Implication: dual-listed/holding-structure discounts (B3 holdings, preferred vs common ON/PN, ADRs) can persist for years -- need catalyst; holding discount not automatically closes.
- Market-level model: two-stage equity DCF; US median ROE stable 12-15%, real GDP growth ~3% since 1945, real cost of equity ~6.5-7% since 1962 (inflation-adjusted). Equity version of formula: P/B = (ROE - g)/(CoE - g) form (replace ROIC with ROE and WACC with cost of equity).

### p05
- End of Ch4 + start of Ch5 (Frameworks for Valuation).
- Market-level intrinsic P/E ~15 (12m forward) historically; ~10 in high-inflation late 1970s/early 80s (market actually fell to ~7, too low); ~25 in 1999 (unjustified by 3% real GDP growth and ROE 12-15%). Deviations corrected in ~3 years. US corporate earnings ~6% of GDP, stable for 40 yrs. Implication: fair market P/E is regime dependent (inflation/rates); B3 structurally lower P/E with high real rates.
- Cap-weighted vs average P/E: S&P trailing P/E 30 in 1999 vs 23 for the other ~95% of firms; 30 largest at 46x. Use median/equal-weight benchmarks. Top-10 tech aggregate P/E 64x in 1999 needed ~one-third of all US corporate profits by 2014 to justify -> sanity check: market-implied scenario vs share of GDP/profit pool ("reverse DCF plausibility").
- Shorting overpriced stocks is costly/risky (noise-trader risk); overvaluation can persist -> a "cheap" screen avoids needing shorts but timing of convergence is uncertain. No fund systematically beat market over 35 yrs (per authors) - mispricing rare, short-lived; require compelling size/duration evidence before acting; most alleged deviations turned out insignificant.
- Ch5 framework: enterprise DCF (FCF at WACC) and economic profit give identical results; APV for changing capital structure; capital cash flow and equity cash flow avoided except for financial institutions (banks/insurers use equity cash flow, capital structure is part of operations).
- Steps: (1) PV of FCF at WACC = value of operations; (2) add nonoperating assets (excess cash, marketable securities, nonconsolidated subs) = enterprise value; (3) subtract ALL nonequity claims; (4) divide equity value by shares. Use UNDILUTED shares when options/converts are valued separately (avoid double count).
- Nonequity claims to subtract: debt (market value if possible), unfunded pensions/retiree health (market prices them even though smoothed on balance sheet; GM example), capitalized operating leases (most common off-balance-sheet debt), contingent liabilities (lawsuits, guarantees), preferred stock (behaves like debt), employee options (Black-Scholes/lattice), minority interest (valued separately, not as % of company value). Enron: hidden debt in unconsolidated subs; stock -50% in a week. Red flag: off-balance-sheet guarantees, unconsolidated subsidiaries.
- Beware double counting: if a pension shortfall is deducted as a claim, do not also model catch-up payments in FCF.
- Home Depot worked example (2003): NOPLAT 5,083; ROIC incl. goodwill 18.2% (avg IC), excl goodwill 18.7%; WACC 9.3% (debt 8.3% of capital at 4.7% pre-tax, 38.2% tax -> 2.9% after tax; cost of equity 9.9%; equity 91.7%). 10y explicit FCF then continuing value 133,360 with g 4%, RONIC = WACC 9.3% (conservative: new investment earns only cost of capital). Mid-year adjustment factor 1.046 (=(1+WACC)^0.5). Operations 79,384 + excess cash 1,609 + other 84 = EV 81,077; less debt 1,365 and capitalized leases 6,554 = equity 73,158 / 2,257m shares = $32.41 vs market $32-38.
- Note: continuing value was 72% of PV of operations (54,757/75,928). Implication: terminal assumptions (g, RONIC) dominate.
- ROIC reorganization: financial statements mix operating, nonoperating and financing items; compute NOPLAT and invested capital after reorganizing. Adjusted EBITA includes adding back operating lease interest (lease capitalized in IC). ROIC uses average invested capital here (some places beginning). Compute ROIC both excluding and including goodwill (HD 18.7% vs 18.2%; Lowe's 14.5% vs 13.9%).
- FCF = NOPLAT + depreciation - gross investment (change in operating WC, net capex, lease investment, intangibles/goodwill, other). FCF is independent of financing: stress test - changing interest rates/dividend policy must not change FCF. Lowe's FCF near zero because reinvesting for growth (not necessarily bad) -> do not penalize low FCF in high-growth high-ROIC firms; check ROIC.
- Forecast horizon structure: short run (line items), medium 5-10y (key drivers: margin, tax, capital efficiency), then continuing value via key value driver formula CV = NOPLAT(t+1)(1 - g/RONIC)/(WACC - g). Typical: growth fading from 10-15% to 5% over 10y.
- WACC = kd(1-Tm) D/(D+E) + ke E/(D+E), market-value weights, target structure. Interest tax shield goes in WACC not in FCF. WACC fails if capital structure changes materially -> use APV (unlevered value + PV tax shields).
- Economic profit model: Value = IC0 + sum IC(t-1)(ROIC_t - WACC)/(1+WACC)^t. Needs beginning-of-year IC and consistent IC definition (ROIC with goodwill needs IC with goodwill). HD: EP 2003 2,645 on spread 10.1%; forecast spread ~8%; operating value 79.4bn vs IC 29.7bn -> value/IC ~2.7x. Model highlights risk: if markets saturate, ROIC falls toward WACC and EP -> 0, while FCF could still grow. Implication: present ROIC-WACC spread and its persistence (competitive advantage period) in the app.
- Simple EP perpetuity: Value = IC0 + EP1/(WACC - g) when RONIC = ROIC; otherwise split current EP (IC x (ROIC-WACC)/WACC) + future EP PV.
- MM logic: with no taxes, swapping debt for equity doesn't change value even though kd < ke (WACC unchanged since cost of equity rises) -> do not treat leverage as a free lunch; cheap debt does not make firm cheaper.

### p06
- End of Ch5 + most of Ch6 (ROIC and Growth) to ROIC-by-industry table start.
- Levered cost of equity: ke = ku + (D/E)(ku - kd) - (Vtxa/E)(ku - ktxa). If ktxa = ku: ke = ku + (D/E)(ku-kd), ku = kd D/(D+E) + ke E/(D+E). Leverage raises ke; cheap debt is not a free lunch. Choose ktxa=ku when debt tracks value (most firms); ktxa=kd when debt level fixed/ distressed / LBO.
- APV = PV(FCF at ku) + PV(interest tax shields); HD ITS = prior net debt x 4.7% x 38.2%; for risky firms haircut tax shields by cumulative default probability (and shields unusable if unprofitable). Net debt = debt + capitalized op leases - excess cash.
- WACC, APV, capital-cash-flow all give same answer under target leverage; authors prefer FCF models since NOPLAT/FCF stay independent of leverage -> cleaner operating comparison across firms.
- Equity cash-flow model: only for financial institutions; risk: raising debt to pay dividends mechanically raises value unless ke adjusted; CV for equity = NetIncome(1 - g/ROE)/(ke - g). HD equity DCF at 9.9% reproduces 73,158.
- Real vs nominal: authors prefer nominal DCF; real projections make ROIC uninterpretable; real only worthwhile in extreme-inflation economies (Ch22). Do NOT discount pretax cash flows at pretax hurdle rate (biased, assumes capex deductible when spent).
- Multiples: use EV/EBITA with comparables; companies differ for valid economic reasons; P/E has capital-structure problems (Ch12 uses forward, capital-structure-independent multiples). Real options: powerful lessons but limited application.
- Ch6: Sanity-check forecasts by ROIC and growth. Example red flag: cost cuts of 3%/yr with price falling 2%/yr in commodity chemicals implies ROIC 8% -> 20%, implausible. Simple 3-driver model: revenue growth, after-tax operating margin, capital turns (ROIC = margin x capital turns). Value with ROIC fading to WACC (10%) by year 7: CV = book invested capital when EP=0 (EV = IC when ROIC=WACC).
- ROIC decomposition: ROIC = (1-T) x (unit price - unit cost) x quantity / IC. Sources of high ROIC: price premium (price setters, brands: Coke ROIC 48% ex goodwill, EV 11x invested capital), cost advantage (Wal-Mart scale, tech), capital efficiency (Southwest aircraft turns). Must identify sustainable advantage (barrier to imitation/entry) to justify high future ROIC. Technology cost improvement shared by all competitors (airline kiosks) is passed to customers -> no ROIC gain.
- Value creation = peak ROIC spread x sustainability (years above WACC). Examples: Intel two cycles (RAM then microprocessors); J&J ROIC > WACC through 30 years (patents + brands + distribution), acquisitions temporarily dampen ROIC; Amgen ~10 years of losses then ROIC ~60% after Epogen approval. Young-company value: depth of losses and time to breakeven.
- EMPIRICAL (5,000+ US nonfinancial firms, 1963-2003, Compustat): median ROIC ex goodwill 9.0% (annual medians 6.9%-10.6%), with goodwill 8.3%; mean ~15.3% (3rd quartile) hmm: interquartile range 5.0%-15.3% (ex goodwill) and 4.7%-13.6% (incl goodwill). Median ROIC moves 20bp per 100bp GDP growth; no upward trend (productivity gains passed to customers/employees). Dispersion widened since 1986 at the top (patent/brand industries) but not when goodwill included (top firms pay full price for acquired performance).
- ROIC distribution: 84% of observations below 20%; cumulative below: <5%: ~25% ... (below 10%: ~56%, 15%: 74... approx: 5->25%? see table: 0:11? use: below 10% = 56%, below 15% = 74%/ ~80% by 17.5? ) - key rule: ROIC > 20% sustained = top ~1 in 6 firms; projecting >20% requires believing company is exceptional.
- Persistence: 50% of firms with ROIC >20% in 1994 still >=20% ten years later; ROICs regress gradually to median but persistent. Median ROIC by industry (ex goodwill, 1963-2003 / 1994-2003): Pharma & Biotech 18.4/21.8; Household & Personal Products 15.2/18.8; Software & Services 15.0/18.1; Media 14.7/14.3; Commercial Services 12.8/13.2; (rest in next part). Basic industries, transportation, utilities 6-8%. Median ROIC differs by industry and growth, NOT by size.
- Growth fades fast: even fastest growers regress to long-run median ~6% real revenue growth within ~5 years; high growth is fleeting.
- Implication for app: benchmark ROIC against sector medians (above) and against the 9% overall median; score persistence (3-5 yr ROIC stability); treat ROIC>20% as exceptional and check moat source; compute ROIC both with and without goodwill to catch acquisition-driven roll-ups.

### p07

Correlation but NOT causation (growth via price cuts lowers ROIC; growth in capacity-constrained sectors keeps prices high until growth slows). No relation between size and ROIC (minimum efficient scale small; Southwest 35% of American's revenue but 8x equity value; Nucor 80% of US Steel revenue, 1.5x value).
- ROIC decay (portfolios by ROIC, 15 yrs): top group (>20%) median falls from ~29% to ~15%, does not fall to the 9% median; low group rises; 5-10% group (regulated) flat. Implication: continuing value assuming ROIC -> WACC is overly conservative for typical high-ROIC firms; consumer staples top group 26% -> 20% after 15 yrs (slower decay); top still beats bottom by >13 pts after 15 yrs. Segment by industry/barrier to entry.
- ROIC 10-year transition probabilities (3y avg ROIC ex goodwill, 1994->2003): from <5%: 43% stay <5, 28% 5-10, 12% 10-15, 6% 15-20, 11% >20. From 5-10%: 31/40/17/7/6. From 10-15%: 21/25/25/11/18. From 15-20%: 18/19/20/17/25. From >20%: 19/5/13/13/50. So high and low ROIC both persistent (~40-50% stay), middle (10-20%) not. Implication: ROIC>20% has 50% chance of being >20% in 10 yrs, 19% chance of falling <5%; persistent ROIC history (several years) is a quality signal; weak firms (<5%) 43% stay weak -> value-trap risk.
- Growth empirics (real revenue growth, 3y rolling, 1963-2003): median 6.3% real (10.2% nominal), range 1.8% (1975) to 10.8% (1998); 3rd quartile 15.4%, 1st quartile -0.2%. Median public-company growth exceeds GDP (3.3%) because of self-selection, outsourcing, foreign revenue, small-firm effect, M&A. One quarter of companies shrink in real terms each year since 1973 -> be skeptical of strong projected growth for mature businesses. Add expected inflation to real figures for forecasts.

- Ch7 key concepts: reorganize statements. ROA/ROE/CFO are biased by nonoperating items and leverage. ROE rises with leverage only when ROIC > after-tax interest rate; falls if ROIC below. Invested capital = operating assets - operating liabilities = debt + equity (+ equivalents) minus nonoperating assets. Total funds invested = IC + nonoperating assets = debt&equivalents + equity&equivalents.
- NOPLAT: EBITA-type operating profit less OPERATING taxes (reported taxes + interest tax shield - taxes on nonoperating income); interest not an operating expense; exclude nonoperating income to match IC. FCF = NOPLAT + noncash opex (depreciation) - gross investment = NOPLAT - net increase in IC. Check: cash flow available to investors reconciles to flows to debt and equity.
- Invested capital components: operating working capital (operating current assets incl WORKING cash only, excl excess cash/securities, less non-interest-bearing operating current liabilities: payables, accrued salaries, deferred revenue, taxes payable); net PP&E; capitalized operating leases; net other operating assets; goodwill/acquired intangibles shown separately. Operating liabilities (payables) are netted against assets, not treated as debt. HD working cash ~2% of sales (1,243 of 64,816; Lowe's 591 of 30,838), excess cash 1,609.
- Goodwill adjustments: add back cumulative amortization/impairments (goodwill does not wear out) and add unrecorded goodwill from old pooling mergers (Lowe's Eagle deal: price ~$1.1bn - equity 370m = 730m). Compute ROIC with AND without goodwill: ex-goodwill measures operating performance; incl-goodwill measures return on total capital paid including acquisitions.
- Long-term operating vs nonoperating: other assets may hide deferred tax assets, prepaid pension assets, nonconsolidated subs; debt equivalents: unfunded pension/retiree medical, restructuring reserves; equity equivalents: deferred taxes, income-smoothing provisions. Hidden assets: operating leases (must capitalize else lessors look capital-light) and expensed investments (R&D, advertising) - capitalize and amortize if possible.
- HD vs Lowe's 2003 numbers: HD NOPLAT 5,083 on IC (incl goodwill) 29,655 -> ROIC ~18.2%; Lowe's NOPLAT 2,223 on 17,012 -> 13.9%.

### p08
- Ch7 practical reorganization + ROIC analysis + revenue growth decomposition (until comps discussion).
- Excess cash: exclude from IC; including it lowers ROIC (HD 18.2% -> 17.4%). Working cash proxy ~2% of sales (S&P 500 firms with smallest cash holdings, 1993-2000); above that = excess; varies by industry (more volatile cash flow -> more cash); in poor shareholder-protection economies firms hoard cash, so median cash overstates need (relevant for B3/EM). Also exclude: nonconsolidated subs, equity investments, customer financing receivables (value separately, remove income from NOPLAT), excess real estate, discontinued ops, prepaid/excess pension assets.
- Debt = ST + LT interest-bearing incl. convertibles. Debt equivalents: unfunded pension/OPEB, capitalized operating leases (retailers like Gap are sub-A rated with minimal formal debt due to leases), restructuring/decommissioning reserves. GM issued $17bn debt (June 2003) to plug pension gap.
- Equity equivalents: deferred taxes, income-smoothing provisions, AOCI; deduct treasury stock. Use CASH taxes (operating taxes minus increase in deferred tax liability) for NOPLAT: HD operating tax rate 37.1% vs cash tax rate 28.6% in 2003 (22.9% of operating taxes deferred). Rising deferrals flatter cash flows but may reverse -> check sustainability of low cash tax rate. Use MARGINAL tax rate (statutory federal + state; HD 38.2%) for tax shields/nonoperating adjustments, not average rate (Disney average tax rate 35-82% vs marginal 37-42%).
- NOPLAT construction: start with EBITA (revenue - COGS - SG&A - depreciation, before goodwill amortization), add back operating-lease interest (embedded in rent) -> adjusted EBITA; subtract operating cash taxes. HD 2003: adj EBITA 7,123, cash taxes 2,040, NOPLAT 5,083. Reconcile to net income: NI + increase in deferred tax + goodwill amortization + after-tax interest (debt and lease) - after-tax nonoperating income - ... = NOPLAT.
- If R&D capitalized: do not deduct R&D from operating profit; deduct amortization of past R&D instead. Stock options are noncash expense: if added back to FCF, value options separately as a claim; if not added back, no separate deduction (consistent treatment).
- FCF components: gross cash flow = NOPLAT + depreciation; gross investment = change in operating working capital + net capex (change in net PP&E + depreciation; do NOT use change in gross PP&E, understates capex when assets retired) + change in capitalized leases + investment in intangibles/goodwill + change in other operating assets - adjust for FX translation (subtract increase in AOCI currency component). Reinvestment ratio = gross investment / gross cash flow; rising without growth = capital inefficiency / projects slow to bear fruit. HD 2003: reinvestment 4,372/6,157=71%; Lowe's 2,719/2,981=91%.
- Some analysts capitalize leases only for ROIC and not FCF; if skipping, do not add back lease interest, no lease change in investment, and do not subtract lease PV as a claim.
- Cash flow available to investors must equal flows to debt + equity (reconciliation check). Combine nonoperating income with changes in nonoperating assets to avoid fake sales (impairment example).
- ROIC rules: numerator and denominator must be consistent (asset in IC => its income in NOPLAT; liability netted in IC => its cost in COGS). Use AVERAGE of beginning and ending IC (starting-only capital overstates ROIC for growing firms; reporting firms often use starting). ROIC better than ROE (mixes leverage) and ROA (double counts supplier financing). HD 2003 18.2% vs Lowe's 13.9% (avg IC incl goodwill); both rose from 2001.
- ROIC with vs without goodwill: with goodwill = return to shareholders on total price paid (value destruction if acquisitions overpaid); without = operating performance for peer comparison. P&G example: ROIC ex goodwill 41.2% vs incl goodwill 21.3% in 2003 (goodwill 13.5bn vs organic IC 13.8bn) - halves ROIC. Implication: serial acquirers need both ratios.
- Economic profit examples: HD 2003 NOPLAT 5.1bn vs capital charge 2.4bn -> EP 2.6bn; EP uses beginning-of-year IC. Profitable (positive NI) firm can destroy value if capital charge > NOPLAT. EP != change in market value: HD TRS $27bn (595m dividends + 26.4bn appreciation) >> EP 2.6bn because price reflects changing expectations.
- ROIC tree: ROIC = (1 - cash tax rate) x EBITA/Revenue x Revenue/IC (margin x capital turns); decompose margin into gross margin, SG&A/revenue, depreciation/revenue; capital turns into operating WC/revenue and fixed assets/revenue. HD vs Lowe's 2003: pretax ROIC 25.5 vs 20.5?; cash tax 28.6% vs 32.5%; HD advantage from capital turns (fixed assets/revenue) partly because Lowe's stores newer/more expensive. Compare with industry peers + industry structure.
- Line-item ratios: use days = 365 x item / revenue (inventory and payables vs COGS to avoid price bias); HD inventory days rose 46 -> 51 while Lowe's fell 60 -> 54 (advantage vanished). Taxes as % of pretax profit, others % of sales. Use quarterly data for seasonal businesses.
- Operational drivers example (airlines): labor/revenue 47.7% vs 25.2% explained by salary per employee ($89.8k vs $43.7k), not productivity (528 vs 525 employees per bn ASM) or yield. Implication: sustainability of margin gaps hinges on structural factors.
- Revenue growth analysis: decompose reported growth into organic, acquisitions, divestitures, currency, accounting changes. IBM 2003: reported +9.8% but organic -2.6% (constant-currency growth 2.8%, acquisitions +5.4%, currency +7.0%). Build pro forma prior-year (partial-year adjustments for acquisitions). Accounting changes: EITF 01-14 reimbursables inflated revenue (TSYS revenue 650 -> 955m, $250m from accounting change, no profit effect); consolidation changes can create artificial growth for years. Implication for app: use organic/constant-currency growth; for B3 exporters/multinationals FX translation distorts growth; BRL depreciation boosts reported revenue of exporters; hyperinflation distort.
- Revenue = units x revenue/unit (price vs mix). Retail: revenue = stores x revenue per store; same-store sales ("comps") growth matters more than new-store growth because new stores need capital (lower turns) while comps need little capital -> raises ROIC. HD: revenue/store $38m vs Lowe's $32.4m; HD growth driven by new stores, not revenue per store. Implication: for retailers use same-store sales and sales/sq m metrics; unit growth with falling per-store revenue = potential ROIC decline.

### p09
- Credit health (Ch7): coverage = EBITA/interest (stricter, assumes replacement capex) and EBITDA/interest (very short-term); EBITDAR/(interest + rent) for lessees/retailers. HD example: EBITA/interest 110x, EBITDAR/(int+rent) 13.4x -> no stress. Implication: use EBITA/interest as primary coverage, not EBITDA; add rent for retail/airlines (B3 retailers).
- ROE = ROIC + (D/E) x [ROIC - (1-T) x kd]; leverage raises ROE but also its sensitivity to ROIC. Assess market D/E vs time and peers. Implication: high ROE with low ROIC = leverage-driven, penalize in quality score; compute ROIC separately from ROE.
- Payout vs reinvestment: high payout + reinvestment ratio >1 = borrowing to pay dividends (unsustainable); low payout + positive FCF = deleveraging or hoarding cash. Value-trap/dividend-trap flag: dividend > FCF.
- History: look back at least 10 years (mean reversion of ROIC); disaggregate ROIC and growth; investigate any radical change (temporary, permanent, accounting).
- Operating leases: capitalized lease value = rent / (kd + 1/asset life) (HD: 570/(4.7%+5%) ~ $5.9bn, 20y life); implied interest = lease value x kd added back to EBITA; tax shield on that interest removed; invested capital += leases; also raise debt in WACC. HD ROIC 22.6% -> 18.2%; Lowe's 16.0% -> 13.9%. Lower ROIC is not lower value creation since WACC falls too. Implication: for B3/US retailers, airlines, comparing EV/EBITDA or ROIC across lessee vs owner needs lease adjustment (post IFRS16/ASC842 lease liabilities now on balance sheet, so EBITDA/net debt must be treated consistently).
- Capitalizing R&D/advertising (outside view): pick amortization life (e.g. 10y), accumulate R&D asset, adjusted EBITA = EBITA + R&D - amortization; do NOT adjust taxes. Merck ROIC 21.5% expensed vs 15.2% capitalized; capitalized R&D ~1/3 of adjusted capital. FCF and valuation unchanged. Implication: ROIC/ROE overstated for pharma/tech/brand-heavy firms; book value understates; P/B and ROIC screens biased against/for them.
- Employee stock options: before 2005 not expensed; estimate cost from pro forma footnote (HD $237m after-tax -> $383m pretax at 38.2%) and deduct from EBITA; no tax or capital adjustment. Yahoo 2003 NI 238m -> 35m (-85%) if expensed. Treat SBC as real cost: do not add back in EBITDA/FCF screens (tech S&P 500).
- Provisions: four types: ongoing operating (returns, warranty: deduct from revenue, net reserve against operating assets), long-term operating (decommissioning: strip implied interest out of EBITA, reserve = debt equivalent), nonoperating restructuring (convert to cash, reserve = debt equivalent), income-smoothing (add back, reserve = equity equivalent). Valuation unaffected by classification, only cash timing matters. Red flag: "other provisions" growing/reversing smooths earnings; check EBITA before vs after provision changes. Use cash taxes (net deferred tax assets/liabilities).
- Pensions: only service cost + prior service cost is operating; interest cost and expected return on plan assets are nonoperating. Lockheed: raw EBITA margin 7.4->6.2% falling, but adjusted 3.5->5.5% rising (2001 pension income inflated EBITA). Unfunded liability = debt equivalent (use footnote PV, not balance sheet); prepaid pension asset nonoperating. Implication: strip pension gains from earnings for legacy industrials; add underfunding to net debt (Brazilian state-company pension funds e.g. Petrobras/Petros, Banco do Brasil/Previ).
- Minority interest: equity equivalent on balance sheet, earnings attributable = financing flow; NOPLAT excludes it. Implication: EV must add minority interest at (market) value; P/E with consolidated profits before minorities is wrong.
- Inflation: ROIC with historic-cost capital inflates in inflation (10% -> 20% when prices double). Fix: revalue PP&E layers by price index, increase depreciation, don't adjust taxes, compare real ROIC with real WACC. Implication: B3/Brazil (high inflation history) and emerging markets: old asset-heavy companies look superior due to cost basis; also book value stale.
- Market vs book capital: ROIC on book is ex-post measure, not for entry/exit decisions (facility with 1% book ROIC may earn 20% on market value of assets).
- CFROI: IRR-style measure (gross invested capital = IC + accumulated depreciation; annual cash flow = NOPLAT + depreciation; residual = return of working capital; fixed over asset life). HD CFROI 20.7%. Use only if lumpy capex, asset life >15y, large fixed assets vs WC. Otherwise stick with ROIC. Taxi example: ROIC rises 10% -> 40% as asset ages while IRR 15% constant; old depreciated assets inflate ROIC.
- Heineken case start: 2003 acquisition + Dutch accounting change (goodwill previously written off to equity, excise duty netting) -> add back cumulative goodwill written off to get capital; use end-of-year capital when acquisition mid-year. Implication: price-to-book/ROE for firms that wrote goodwill off directly to equity is distorted; compute ROIC incl. goodwill (ex and incl.) for acquisitive firms.

Forecast operating taxes on EBITA first, then back into reported taxes, so ROIC/FCF do not vary with leverage. Gap between operating and marginal rate often from credits; if credits won't grow with EBITA, tax at marginal rate. Implication: use normalized operating tax rate (not last-year effective, which may be one-off) in NOPLAT/ROIC; B3 firms with JCP (juros sobre capital proprio) shields and tax incentives need care.

Capital structure in model does not affect enterprise DCF value except through WACC; ROIC and FCF should not change if payout ratio varied (model check). Implication: debt/cash balance is irrelevant to EV; only net debt at valuation date matters for equity bridge.

- Heineken medium-term: steady state 2009+: growth 3% (1.5 vol + 1.5 price), adj EBITA margin 12.5%, WC/revenue 6.7%, PP&E/revenue 54.6%, turnover 1.7x, ROIC 15% ex-goodwill, WACC 7.5%. Review Q IMT (International Machine Tools) exercise: ignore.
- Ch9 Continuing value (CV). CV often 56%-125% of total value with 8-year explicit forecast (tobacco, sporting goods, skin care, high tech). Large CV share does not mean value created late: early FCF is depressed by growth investment. Interpret via business components (Innovation Inc.: base business 71% of value; new product line 29%) or via economic profit (62% of value = invested capital in place). Implication: "CV share" is not a red flag per se; but check that CV assumptions (g, RONIC) are defensible.
- Value driver formula: CV = NOPLAT(t+1) x (1 - g/RONIC) / (WACC - g). Equivalent to FCF perpetuity if FCF is set right. Example: NOPLAT 100, g 6%, RONIC 12%, WACC 11% -> CV 1,000. Parameter rules: NOPLAT normalized at midpoint of cycle (cycle-average margin); RONIC = WACC for most companies (competition erodes), but = last explicit-year ROIC for firms with durable advantages (brands, patents); g = long-run consumption growth of the industry's products + inflation, not above economy; WACC with sustainable capital structure.
- Sensitivity (WACC 10%, NOPLAT 100): at RONIC 14%, g 6% -> 8% lifts CV 50% (1.4bn -> 2.1bn). Implication: app should show sensitivity grid rather than point value; keep g <= nominal GDP (Brazil ~ inflation + 1-2% real; US ~ 2-3% total in 2004 dollars era; now use bond yield cap).
- Economic profit CV: EP(t+1)/WACC + [NOPLAT(t+1) x g/WACC? i.e. NOPLAT(t+1)(g/RONIC)(RONIC-WACC)] / [WACC(WACC-g)]; total value = IC + PV(EP). Same answer as DCF. Heineken: IC 10,787 + EP CV 18,387 = CV 29,173 (NOPLAT2019 1,641; g 3%; RONIC 15%; WACC 7.5%). Value = IC + PV of economic profit -> P/B logic: justified P/B (or EV/IC) rises with ROIC-WACC spread and growth.
- Forecast horizon doesn't change value if steady state reached: same $893 with 5-25 year horizons (CV share 79% at 5y, 60% at 10y ... 26% at 25y). Horizon matters only if it hides inconsistent assumptions (e.g., RONIC>WACC in explicit period then =WACC in CV). Explicit period must be at least as long as any margin trend (margins 12% -> 9% over 7 years needs >=7y).
- Competitive advantage period misconception: RONIC=WACC in CV does NOT mean ROIC on total capital goes to WACC; base capital keeps earning its last-year ROIC. Example: base ROIC 18%, RONIC 10%, g 4.5%: average ROIC falls to 14% after 11 years, 12% after 23, 11% after 37 -> fade is gradual.
- Pitfalls: (1) naive base-year extrapolation: growing year-10 FCF by new g keeps reinvestment too high (FCF understated ~18%); recompute investment = WC at constant % of sales; (2) naive overconservatism: RONIC=WACC understates brand cos (Coca-Cola, Pepsi, pharma, consumer, some software); (3) purposeful conservatism: model uncertainty with scenarios, not biased inputs. Implication for app: using a flat low/hardcoded multiple for quality franchises (e.g. Ambev, Weg, Coca-Cola) understates; but for commodity/cyclical, RONIC=WACC is right.
- Other CV methods: book value, liquidation (80% WC, 70% fixed assets; only if liquidation likely or dying industry), P/E or M/B multiples (circular; sporting goods example CV dispersion 186 to 624 by method), replacement cost (understates intangibles; overstates when assets uneconomic to replace), perpetuity of last FCF. Don't set exit P/E = P/E paid today; the right multiple at horizon depends on then-g, RONIC, WACC. Convergence formula CV = NOPLAT/WACC (RONIC = WACC; does not imply zero growth). "Aggressive growth" formula CV = NOPLAT/(WACC - g) with nominal g (inflation) implies RONIC -> infinity (growth with no capital) -> overstates. Never use NOPLAT/(WACC-g) without reinvestment.
- Two-stage CV (g_A, RONIC_A for N years, then g_B, RONIC_B) available; g_B < WACC required. Declining ROIC on all capital should be modeled in explicit period, not in CV formula.
- Ch10 Cost of capital: WACC = kd(1-Tm) D/V + ke E/V, with target market-value weights (not book), after-tax, same currency and nominal/real as cash flows; excludes operating liabilities (payables) -- their cost is in operating expenses. Use unlevered cost of equity + APV when capital structure changes drastically (LBO). Home Depot: D/V 8.3%, kd 4.7%, tax 38.2%, ke 9.9% (rf 4.3% 10y govt, MRP 4.5%, relevered industry beta 1.23) -> WACC 9.3%. Cost of debt = YTM on long-term debt (or from rating), not coupon. Implication: app WACC for B3 in BRL nominal: Selic/NTN-B-based rf, with ERP + country risk; US in USD; never compare yields across currencies.

### p13
- Cost of equity (Ch10): CAPM ke = rf + beta x MRP. Beta examples: General Mills 0.4 -> ke 6.3% (rf 4.3, MRP 5); Cisco 1.4 -> 11.3%. Defensive staples low beta, tech/cyclicals high.
- Risk-free: long-term (10y) local-currency government yield (zero-coupon strip ideal), NOT T-bill (0.9% vs 4.3% in Dec 2003); in Europe use 10y German bund; match currency of cash flows. Implication: B3 valuations use BRL long yield (NTN-F/ Tesouro prefixado 10y) as rf and USD 10y UST for S&P 500; do not mix.
- MRP: authors' range 4.5-5.5% (just under 5% at 2003). Historical (US 1903-2002): 6.2% arithmetic, 4.4% geometric vs long bonds; arithmetic compounded is biased upward (estimation error, negative autocorrelation); Blume estimator R = ((T-N)/(T-1))RA + ((N-1)/(T-1))RG; multi-year holding-period excess returns decline 6.2% (1y) -> 5.5% (10y). Survivorship bias: subtract 1-2% => 3.5-4.5% future US premium. Textbook ~8% too high (short bonds, 75 yrs, US strength). Forward-looking: ke = CFe/P approach with ROE 13% and real growth 3.5% gives constant real market return ~7.0% (US), 6.0% (UK) => MRP = 7.0% real - TIPS 2.1% = just under 5%. Dividend-yield regressions can give negative premium (late 1990s).
- Beta: raw beta from regression of monthly stock returns on diversified (S&P 500 / MSCI World) index, 60 months (5y monthly), not daily/weekly (illiquidity zeros bias beta down; bid-ask bounce). Do NOT use local index (concentrated in few sectors/companies) -> implication for Brazil: Ibovespa heavy in Vale/Petrobras/banks so beta vs Ibovespa is sector exposure, not market risk; consider global index or industry betas. Plot rolling beta for structural change (IBM 0.7 -> 1.3). HD raw beta 1.37, std error 0.20, R2 0.43 (range 0.97-1.77) => single-stock betas noisy.
- Bubble distortion: TMT share of S&P 500 20% -> ~50% (1999-2001) pushed non-TMT betas to ~0 (food 0.85 -> 0.02); exclude bubble years when estimating beta. Implication: betas estimated during sector-dominated markets mislead.
- Improve beta: industry betas: unlever each (beta_u = beta_e / (1 + D/E), D = net debt incl. operating leases less excess cash, market equity), take median/average across peers (HD 1.27, Lowe's 1.02 -> industry 1.14), relever at target D/E (HD 1.23, Lowe's 1.30). Assumes debt beta 0 and tax-shield beta = beta_u. Smoothing: Bloomberg adjusted beta = 0.33 + 0.67 x raw; better: weight on raw = sigma_b^2/(sigma_e^2+sigma_b^2) (HD adjusted 1.33; cross-sectional beta SD 0.59).
- Unlevered cost of equity = CAPM on unlevered industry beta (for APV).
- Fama-French 3-factor: Ri - rf = a + b1(Rm-rf) + b2 SMB + b3 HML; premia SMB 3.0%, HML 4.4%; HD ke 9.8% vs CAPM 10.4% (large, growth stock -> lower). Small-cap and high book-to-market (value) tilt historically earned excess returns, but SMB premium vanished after 1982. Implication for app: value (high B/P) premium is a compensation for risk per FF, but empirical not theory-based; don't overweight small caps solely for size; cheapness on B/P alone is documented premium but includes distressed stocks.
- APT: multi-factor; unimplemented practically (academic).

### p14
- CAPM vs FF critique: Kothari-Shanken-Sloan argue tests had low power, annual-return betas show beta-return link, B/M premium may be survivorship bias; FF factors vanish when controlling for leverage. Authors keep CAPM. Implication: "cheap on B/P" premium evidence is contested; leverage correlates with size and B/M, so cheap high-B/P stocks tend to be levered.
- Cost of debt: YTM of long-term, option-free, liquid bonds (investment grade BBB or better); else rating-based spread over govt (Dec 2003 10y spreads: AAA 28bp, AA 34, A 48, BBB 102, BB 236, B 303 bp). Never use coupon rate. HD: 4.34% + 34bp = 4.68%.
- Below investment grade: YTM overstates expected return (example: 25% default prob, 50% recovery, kd 6% -> YTM 21.1%). Use BBB YTM + ~0.5% for BB and below (bond betas: Treasury 0.19, IG 0.27, HY 0.37). Implication: for distressed/high-yield names do not plug yield into WACC; flag.
- After-tax kd = kd(1-Tm); statutory marginal rate overstates the realizable rate for the typical firm (Graham: ~5 pts lower on average; tax-loss carryforwards); for profitable investment-grade use statutory.
- WACC weights: target market-value D/V and E/V (not book, not current if temporary). Target via: current market structure, comparable companies, management philosophy (UPS conservative). Median debt/value 2003 by industry: IT 0%, healthcare 4, aerospace 12, industrial machinery 13, consumer discretionary 15, staples 19, oil and gas 22, chemicals/paper/metals 26, telecom 30, airlines 33, utilities 47; S&P 500 median D/V 13.1%, D/E 19.7%. Asset-heavy, stable industries carry more debt; intangible/high-growth ones little. Implication: leverage thresholds must be sector-relative (utilities 47% is normal; software 0%).
- Debt market value ~ book unless rates moved or distress; include operating leases (and pensions) in debt only if FCF is adjusted consistently. Equity = price x shares outstanding (not issued). Minority interest valued at peer P/E x minority income (Heineken 15.6x). Circular for private companies: iterate.
- Hybrids/convertibles: if materially affecting value use APV; if unlikely converted = debt, if deep in the money = equity; else split bond + call option.
- Heineken WACC 7.5%: target 10% D/V (current 24% inflated by acquisitions; history <10%), kd 4.5% (40bp spread over euro rf 4.1%), ke 8.0% (rf 4.1 + beta 0.75 x MRP 5.2%), beta from brewer unlevered median 0.66 (range 0.53-0.86; consumer staples low beta). Market value equity 13.2bn vs book 3.2bn. Implication: defensive beverage/consumer -> low beta, low WACC, supports higher multiples; app can use sector unlevered betas.
- Ch11 Calculating results: value of operations = PV(FCF) + PV(CV); discount CV by N years (not N+1); midyear adjustment: multiply value of operations by (1+WACC)^0.5. Time-varying WACC discount factor = product of (1+WACC_t). For big capital structure shifts use APV.
- Equity bridge (Exhibit 11.1): value of operations + excess cash + nonconsolidated subsidiaries + loans + tax loss carryforwards + discontinued ops + excess real estate = enterprise value; minus debt (bank loans, bonds), minus debt equivalents (operating leases, unfunded pensions, preferred, long-term operating provisions, nonoperating provisions, contingent liabilities), minus hybrids (employee options, convertibles), minus minority interests = equity value. Example: 5,000 -> EV 5,365 -> equity 3,450 (EV overstates equity by 36%). Implication: app EV-to-equity bridge must include leases, pensions, minorities, preferred, options; net debt alone is incomplete (esp. B3: minority interests, Petros/Previ-type pensions).
- Valuing items: excess cash at book/market (if discounting securities, use rf not WACC); loans at book if fair terms; nonconsolidated subs: market value if listed (check liquidity/free float), else DCF/ P/E x net income (20-50% stakes) or tracking portfolio (<20%); equity method for 20-50%, cost method <20%. Holding companies: sum-of-parts of stakes -> relevant to B3 holdings (Itausa, Bradespar, etc.): discount for holding structure not covered here.
- Tax loss carryforwards: value separately, discount tax savings at kd (or unlevered ke if large relative to profits); ongoing deferred tax assets are not NOLs. Discontinued ops at fair value (net asset on BS). Excess real estate by appraisal. 
- Distressed firms: debt trades below book and moves with EV; deducting market value of debt overstates equity; use scenario approach: for each scenario, EV - full debt/claims, floor equity at 0, weight by probability. Convertibles/options: deduct at value implied by DCF (higher than market if DCF > price). Implication: equity as call option on assets for distressed names; an "undervalued" signal in leveraged distressed names is unreliable.

### p15
- Debt equivalents in the EV-to-equity bridge: operating leases (capitalized = rent/(kd + 1/life)), unfunded pensions/OPEB at fair value after tax (use footnote funded status, not balance sheet; example: pension underfunded 266 + OPEB 333 = 599 pre-tax = 389 after tax at 35%; balance sheet showed pension as 437 net ASSET). Unfunded US pension total $314bn (2002), concentrated in autos, aerospace/defense, airlines, integrated oil, utilities; 144 companies with unfunded >10% of EV. Defined-contribution plans irrelevant; defined-benefit are debt. Avoid double counting pension assets inside "other assets".
- Preferred stock = debt equivalent (value at market, or perpetuity of dividends at cost of unsecured debt). Provisions in bridge: ongoing operating (warranty/returns) NOT deducted (already in FCF); long-term operating (decommissioning) deduct at book; nonoperating (restructuring) deduct at book; income-smoothing NOT deducted. Contingent liabilities (litigation, guarantees): expected after-tax cash flow discounted at kd, test a range of probabilities. Implication: B3 contingencias (tax/labor/civil litigation provisions, e.g. Petrobras, Brazilian banks) are debt-like claims; flag provisions growth.
- Employee stock options: deduct value of vested (and probability-weighted unvested) options as nonequity claim using Black-Scholes/binomial value from footnote; exercise-value method (treat as exercised, add proceeds) overstates (109.0 vs 105.8 per share in example; ignoring options entirely gives 111.1, 5% overstatement). Future grants go in FCF as expense. Implication: for S&P tech with heavy SBC, per-share value must use diluted count or option value; do not add back SBC.
- Convertible debt = straight bond + call option; example bond value 108.2 (81.3 straight + 26.9 option) vs 100 face; per-share 99.1 (option method) vs 102 (conversion method) vs 100 nondiluted. Deep in-the-money -> treat as equity (conversion value, divide by diluted shares). Use undiluted shares if claims deducted at option value; treasury shares reduce share count (some firms show as asset).
- Minority interest: deduct market value of outsiders' stake in the subsidiary (valued on the subsidiary, not the group); listed carve-out -> market price x outsiders' stake.
- Scenario valuation: build complete bridge per scenario (debt valued at recovery value in downside); example double-or-quit product launch: success EV ops 5,522 / equity 2,635, failure ops 2,165 / equity 0 (debt worth 2,406 vs face 3,500); probability weighted (67/33) equity 1,766; deducting debt at face in a probability-weighted EV would understate equity by 361. A change in success probability (2/3 -> 3/4) moves value 12%; cost cuts irrelevant in downside. Implication: levered companies with binary outcomes -> equity is an option; don't use net debt book values blindly.
- Model integrity checks: balance sheet balances; NOPLAT top-down = bottom-up; ROIC > WACC => value of operations > book invested capital; compare with key-value-driver back-of-envelope; avoid step changes causing negative capex; steady state reached at CV date. Sensitivity: if ROIC ~ WACC then value insensitive to growth. Plausibility: assume market is right by default unless low free float/liquidity; compare implied multiples (EV/EBITA) to peers and explain differences.
- Art of valuation (rules of thumb): firm with forward P/E 15-16: +0.5 pt WACC => -10% value; +1 pt growth for 15 years => +6%; sensitivity highest when interest rates low and for high-growth firms; typical stock vol 25% => monthly 95% range around +-15%. Authors target valuation range +-15%. Implication: app margin of safety should be at least ~15-20%; small score differences are noise; treat rank differences within ~15% as ties.
- Heineken valuation: value of ops EUR 16.855bn (incl. midyear factor 1.04 = (1.075)^0.5); EV 19.237bn; equity 13.466bn = EUR 34.35/share vs market 33.65. Value of ops ~3x invested capital (ROIC 15% = 2x WACC). Zero growth => EV/IC = ROIC/WACC. CV (29.173bn) discounted 10bn = ~60% of value. Scenarios: operating improvement EUR 46.97 (ROIC 21%, +37%), business as usual 34.35, aggressive acquisitions 24.55 (-29%); probabilities 25/60/15 -> 36.03 (+7% to market). Spread of values +-30% even for stable consumer firm. Sensitivity: WACC -0.5pt => +17.5%; margin +1pt => +13%; organic growth +1pt => +11.5%; capital turnover +0.1x => +1.1%. Margins more achievable than growth in mature markets.
- Nonconsolidated participating interests valued at brewer multiple 16x income (480m); excess cash at book; financial fixed assets (loans) at book.
- Ch12 Multiples start: comps table HD forward P/E 13.3 (33.00/2.48), Lowe's 14.4, BBBY 19.1, Linens 12.1, Best Buy 13.8, Circuit City 22.3 (depressed EPS), S&P 500 15.6; forward EV/EBITDA 4.4-9.9 for hardline retail. Authors: multiples are useful to test DCF and explain differences, but industry-average P/E ignores differing growth, ROIC, capital structure; P/E mixes operating and nonoperating items; a good multiples analysis requires DCF-like adjustments.

### p16
- Ch12 Multiples (core for app). Growth drives multiples only if ROIC > WACC. Derived: EV/EBITA = (1-T)(1 - g/ROIC)/(WACC - g). Four drivers: growth, ROIC, cash tax rate, WACC. When ROIC = WACC, EV/EBITA = (1-T)/WACC regardless of growth (30% tax, 9% WACC -> 7.8x). Table (T 30%, WACC 9%): g 4.0%: ROIC 6/9/15/20/25 -> 4.7/7.8/10.3/11.2/11.8x; g 6.0%: n/a/7.8/14.0/16.3/17.7x. Same multiple from (g 6%, ROIC 15%) as (g 5%, ROIC 25%). At ROIC < WACC higher growth lowers multiple. Implication: cheap-vs-expensive must be judged against ROIC-WACC spread and growth; a "low multiple" with ROIC<WACC is deserved. Use this formula to compute an implied/justified EV/EBITA per stock and compare with actual (cheapness = justified minus actual).
- HD discount vs peers shrinks with better method: trailing P/E vs all hardline retailers -19%; vs Lowe's only -17%; forward P/E vs Lowe's -8%; forward EV/EBITA -7%; EV/EBITA adjusted for leases and excess cash -5%. A 19% apparent undervaluation was mostly an artifact. Implication: avoid trailing P/E vs broad sector average.
- Four best practices: (1) comparables with similar ROIC and growth prospects; (2) forward-looking (not trailing) estimates; (3) EV/EBITA rather than P/E; (4) adjust EV and EBITA for nonoperating items (excess cash, operating leases, employee options, pensions).
- Peer group: SIC/NAICS poor; GICS better at explaining multiples, growth, R&D, ratios (Bhojraj-Lee-Oler). Multi-segment company needs separate peer group per segment (use sum-of-parts). Representative multiple: use median or harmonic mean (reciprocal of average EBITA/EV), not arithmetic mean (distorted by temporarily low earnings -> huge multiples). Exclude negative-earnings comps (or move up to EBITDA/sales sparingly). Implication for app: sector-relative z-scores should use median or harmonic mean of earnings yields; ranking by earnings yield (E/P, EBITA/EV) rather than P/E handles negatives and outliers more robustly.
- Forward vs historical evidence: historical E/P std dev 6.0% vs forward 3.7% (1.6x); median pricing error 23% (historical), 18% (1-yr forward), 16% (2-yr forward) (Liu-Nissim-Thomas). IPO study: pricing error 55% (historical) -> 43.7% (1y fwd) -> 28.5% (2y fwd); share within 15%: 15.4% -> 18.9% -> 36.4%. Caveat: forward multiples derived by analysts backing out from industry multiple add no information. If only historical, use latest and strip one-offs. Implication: app should prefer forward consensus EPS/EBITA when available, normalized trailing otherwise; blend to reduce error.
- P/E flaws: depends on leverage and includes nonoperating gains/losses. P/E vs leverage (no taxes, kd 5%): unlevered P/E 10 -> falls with debt (9.5 at 10% D/V down to 6.7 at 50%); 20 -> constant; 25/40 -> rises with leverage (40 at 50% D/V not meaningful). Rule: leverage raises P/E for high-multiple (growth) firms and lowers it for low-multiple firms; crossover where unlevered P/E = 1/kd. Implication: low P/E among highly levered low-growth firms understates their cheapness gap in reverse; compare P/E only among similarly levered firms; prefer EV/EBITA. AOL Time Warner 2002: EBITA $6.4bn but $98bn net loss from write-offs -> P/E meaningless.
- EV multiple adjustments: EV = debt + equity - excess cash (+ capitalized leases + outstanding option value + after-tax pension deficit), EBITA = reported + implied lease interest (+ pension interest - recognized plan returns, - option grant expense). Circuit City: P/E 22.3 looked expensive but equity 2,708 included ~990 cash; ex-cash P/E 15.1 and EV/EBITDA 4.4 cheapest. Best Buy EV 6.3x premium but P/E 13.8x discount -> contradictory signals resolved by cash. Rule: P/E of cash is ~50 (2% after-tax yield), inflating P/E of cash-rich firms. Implication: strip net cash before judging P/E for cash-rich firms (tech S&P 500, Brazilian exporters); app should compute EV-based and cash-adjusted multiples.
- HD adjusted: EV 75,615 + leases 6,554 - excess cash 1,609 = 80,560; EBITA 8,691 + lease interest 340 = 9,031; EV/EBITA 8.9x vs Lowe's 9.4x (raw 8.7 vs 9.3).
- EBITDA vs EBITA: EBITDA ignores capex needs; own-vs-outsource example: A (owns equipment) EBITDA 50 EV/EBITDA 3.0; B (outsources) EBITDA 25 EV/EBITDA 6.0; both EBITA 20 and EV/EBITA 7.5 identical. EBITDA multiples misleading across business models/asset intensity; EBITDA-capex adjustment subjective, can be negative. Depreciation tax shield also creates value differences. Implication: prefer EV/EBITA (or EV/(EBITDA - maintenance capex)); EV/EBITDA only within asset-similar peers.
- Price-to-sales: weak (extra assumption of similar margins). Circuit City 0.1x sales vs Bed Bath & Beyond 1.7x -> HD implied $4 to $60/share. Use only if earnings negative (VC); use EV/sales, forward, similar ROIC/margin peers.
- PEG: adjusted PEG = EV multiple / (100 x expected EBITA growth). HD 0.60 vs Lowe's 0.42 (but HD ROIC 17% vs 14%: PEG ignores ROIC). Flaws: no standard growth horizon; linear with zero intercept so undervalues low-growth firms (a zero growth company is worth 0); biased against low-growth stocks. Regression EV/EBITA = 3.5 + 0.2 x growth(%) for retailers (zero growth = 3.5x). Implication: avoid PEG as primary; if used, regress multiples on growth and ROIC within sector rather than ratio.
- Nonfinancial multiples (per customer/page view) were useful in 1990s internet only until financials became predictive (gross profit and R&D later); relative not absolute; only if tied to ROIC and growth. Summary: DCF best; multiples as a careful check with as much care as DCF; never a shortcut.
- Ch13 Performance measurement start: framework performance (ROIC, growth, economic profit) vs health (organic growth drivers, innovation, customers, risk management) vs market (TRS, market value added). EPS growth is a poor measure: can come from heavy investment or leverage even if ROIC < WACC. Economic profit = IC x (ROIC - WACC) adds size: A (ROIC 20%, IC 10bn) EP 1bn vs B (30%, 2bn) EP 400m. HD 5-yr avg ROIC 15.6% vs WACC 9.2%, highest among large US retailers; many pharma, consumer packaged goods, industrials have ROIC >25%. Stock price alone misleading (oil company's price follows oil; bank's follows rates; HD price fell 1999-2003 though value creation high). Historical metrics subjective and don't capture short-term vs long-term trade-offs -> health metrics.

### p17 (Ch13 end: retail ROIC/EP, EPS critique, health, TRS; Ch14 start)
- Compare ROIC/growth only within the right peer set (Home Depot vs retailers, not all industries); retail 5y ROIC (op-lease adjusted) ranged 4-16%, young retailers mid-teens vs old ones below cost of capital. Winners = high ROIC AND growth; old retailers shrinking (growth ~0 or negative) with ROIC under WACC = value-trap pattern.
- Economic profit = (ROIC - WACC) x invested capital; ranks differ from ROIC: size matters. Best Buy high ROIC/growth but small capital, so less EP than HD/Wal-Mart. Implication: score both ROIC-WACC spread and EP/market cap, not ROIC alone.
- EPS is a poor quality/performance metric: ignores cost of equity, gameable via financing. Example: $1bn debt-funded acquisition at 6% debt, 40% tax, +$100m EBIT -> net income +$24m but EP -$30m (NOPAT ROIC 6% < 9% WACC).
- Break-even: acquisition needs after-tax return >= WACC (9% => $90m after tax, $150m pretax at 40% tax).
- Implied P/E on debt = 1/[r_d x (1-t)] (6%, 40% tax => 27.8x). Buyback raises EPS whenever buy P/E < that; so EPS growth from buybacks is not value creation. Flag: EPS growth driven by share count shrink and one-offs (Sears EPS +34% from asset-sale gains and buybacks; another firm ~20%/yr EPS growth vs 4%/yr operating profit growth).
- Implication for app: use operating profit/NOPAT growth, ROIC, FCF, not EPS growth; adjust for one-time items and share count changes; stock price tends not to follow EPS growth that isn't operating.
- Still, strong growth plus high ROIC will show up in EPS; the issue is 1-3 year horizons being manipulable.
- Health metrics (short/medium/long term): sales productivity (same-store sales, price/volume, share), op cost productivity (unit cost), capital productivity (working capital, PP&E turns); medium: pipeline, brand, customer satisfaction, cost structure, asset age (remodel cycle); long: strategic threats, organization. Declining health (underinvestment in stores/brand, patent cliffs: drug sales fall 50-75% after patent expiry) can hide behind strong current margins -> cheap-looking earnings may be peak (value trap).
- Separate management-controlled from external effects (e.g., oil price windfalls, real estate prices): cyclical/commodity profits are not skill; judge by reserves, production growth, costs.
- Home Depot example: gross margin 30.9% to 33.4%, ROIC 14.9% to 18.2%, comps +3.5%, store count +13.4%/yr; market saturation (78% of superstore market) constrains growth.
- TRS = price change + dividends; mostly reflects change in expectations ("expectations treadmill"): great companies can give ordinary returns; low-expectation companies easy to beat. Good company != good investment short-term. TRS is highly dependent on start date (1999 near market peak; HD TRS -2.3%/yr 1999-2003 despite strong EP).
- MVA = market value of debt+equity minus invested capital; Market-value-to-capital = (D+E)/IC. HD: MV $88bn (8 debt/leases + 80 equity), IC $29bn => MVA $59bn, MV/IC 3.1. Retail MV/IC range 0.7 (Dillard's) to 3.5 (Wal-Mart, Best Buy); weak ones 1.0-1.8 with low ROIC. MV/IC is a relative expectations gauge; pairing with ROIC is the idea (high MV/IC only justified with ROIC>>WACC and growth).
- Treadmill matrix: MV/capital (x) vs TRS (y), quadrants relative to median.
- Reverse DCF: solve what growth/ROIC the price implies, then judge feasibility. HD 2003: price consistent with ROIC ~18-19% held and growth fading 12% (2004) to 5% (2013); equity DCF $73bn; implied ~8%/yr growth for 10y; in 1998 price implied 26%/yr growth (triple stores) -> expectations were unrealistic, explaining poor later TRS. Implication: implement reverse-DCF "implied growth" indicator; flag stocks whose prices need growth beyond saturation.
- Ch14 (Performance Management) start: VBM history; early EVA programs failed by being short-term and measurement-only. Mostly governance, low app relevance. Value drivers/KPIs: top 10-15 metrics. Review questions mostly irrelevant.

### p18 (Ch14 value drivers/targets/reviews/compensation; Ch15 M&A start)
- Ch14 is mostly management-process content; low direct app relevance. Useful nuggets only.
- Value drivers differ by business model: commodity chemicals -> cost and uptime; branded consumer goods -> loyalty; price retailers -> supply chain cost, convenience retailers -> availability. Implication: indicator weights should differ by sector.
- Trade-off short vs long term: R&D/advertising expensed immediately depress current returns; cutting them boosts near-term margins (red flag when margin gains coincide with falling R&D/capex/ad spend).

- Case: factory-availability overemphasis during high-margin period led to over-maintenance/redundant capex that drained returns when margins normalized (cyclical caution: peak-margin earnings).

- Benchmarks: external peers, internal, own history (percentiles of own performance), theoretical limits. Implication: compare each indicator vs sector peers AND own history.
- Compensation: stock comp gets inflated by macro tailwinds (declining rates in late 1990s) - not skill. Low app relevance.

Internal investment usually returns more per dollar than acquisitions.

- "Undervalued by market" claims are usually rationalizations; real mispricings are short-lived: overreaction to bad news, cyclical bottoms (buying at trough and selling at peak could more than double returns). Late-1990s TMT acquirers saw share prices collapse; M&A rises after strong markets -> overpaying risk at high valuations.

### p19 (Ch15 M&A synergies, payment, accretion; Ch16 divestitures start)
- Synergy realism (McKinsey study of 90 deals): 88% captured >=70% of cost synergies; only ~half captured >=70% of revenue synergies; ~1/4 captured <30%. Implication: discount acquisition-story growth; revenue-synergy narratives are unreliable; cost synergies more credible.
- Merger disrupts customers, lets competitors poach staff, dual-source customers shift, discount demands -> baseline revenue often falls. Low-growth buying high-growth does not create value by itself.
- Skeptical of "lower cost of capital" synergies from diversification (investors diversify themselves). Conglomerate/holding discount logic.
- Implementation costs can equal a year or more of synergies; synergies not captured in first full budget year often never are; many savings are temporary (excess capacity disappears with industry growth).
- Top-down premium test (target: rev 1,000, margin 10%, tax 40%, NOPAT 60, IC 300, ROIC 20%, WACC 10%, MV 800 => implied g = 4%). A 30% premium (price 1,040) needs +2.4 pts margin (about +25% operating profit in perpetuity) OR +1.9 pts perpetual growth. Method: reverse DCF of growth/margin combos. Implication for app: implied-growth reverse DCF is a standard tool.
- Cash vs stock: stock shares risk with target holders; cash deals bear full risk. Stock use signals possible overvaluation of acquirer (issuance signal); bubble -> pay in stock. Example: automotive supplier paid cash for serial acquisitions, synergies failed, debt unsustainable -> bankruptcy. Red flag: debt-funded serial acquirers.
- Accretion/dilution is irrelevant to value: goodwill amortization eliminated by 2005 (IFRS/US GAAP), so cash deals are mostly EPS-accretive. Example: buy $400m company for $500m cash, target NI 30 vs after-tax interest 19.5 (6% pretax, 35% tax = 3.9% after-tax) -> EPS 2.0 -> 2.3 yet value destroyed (ROIC 6% < WACC). Stock deal accretive if acquirer P/E > target P/E (EPS is weighted average). Study of 117 deals >$3bn (1999-2000): accretive vs dilutive had no difference in market reaction (~41% positive). Implication: EPS growth from acquisitions with leverage is low quality; focus on ROIC on acquired capital > WACC.
- Successful acquirers: strong core business ("two dogs do not make a lion"), proactive screening, disciplined price, early integration. GE example: industrial ROIC (ex-goodwill) rose from <20% to >50%, organic growth ~ GDP.
- Winner's curse: if rivals dropped out, ask why. Rising premium during negotiation.
- Ch16 divestitures: announcement returns positive: mean +3.0% (spin-offs 4.5%, carve-outs 2.3%, asset sales 2.6%); 3-day CAR from 370 deals. Active portfolio managers (divest + acquire) beat passive: $100 (1990-1999) grew to $519 balanced vs $353 passive (divester $442? acquirer $392). Companies divest too late; 75% of divestitures reactive; >60% of companies did <=2 in a decade. General Dynamics: divested after 1991, revenue halved, TSR 58%/yr 1991-95, then >20%/yr after buying selectively.
- Divestiture dilutes EPS (interest on cash 2% or debt 6% < earnings yield of divested unit) but raises equity value and P/E. Example: unit worth 400 to parent sold for 500; equity value 1,900 -> 2,000; EPS falls from 1.50 to 1.21-1.34 (cash/debt) but P/E rises 12.7 -> 15-16.6; buyback keeps EPS 1.52 at P/E 13.1. Implication: do not penalize EPS drop from disposals; P/E comparisons across restructured firms are distorted.

### p20 (Ch16 divestitures end; Ch17 Capital Structure start)
- Divestiture + buyback is EPS-dilutive if sale price/earnings of unit < P/E of remaining business; divestitures of mature units often dilute EPS yet raise value. Divestiture waves follow new-CEO tenure (about half of major divestitures at early-tenure CEOs).
- Value from divesting: unit worth more to another owner; hidden costs of holding mature units (culture, diverted management time, cyclical business confuses investors - Pactiv sold aluminum 1999). Conglomerate/holding discount logic: parent must be "natural owner".
- Relative TRS vs industry declines with business age (Exhibit 16.5); TRS can fall even with growth, as valuations decline (paper industry sales growth positive but excess TRS over S&P negative). Implication: growth alone does not mean good stock; mature, asset-heavy industries (paper, steel, cement) show declining relative returns.
- Spin-offs: operating margin of spun-off subsidiary improves ~1/3 within three years; 2y market-adjusted TRS positive (subs +13.1% avg, parents +3.1%); gains concentrated in focus-improving spin-offs (+8.6% vs -1.9% non-focus). Carve-outs: avg market-adjusted long-term performance negative; those from distressed parents negative; most (92%) do not stay majority-controlled; those with parent holding >25% stakes lingering returned about -17 to -32% median. Tracking stock destroys value. Examples: Wanadoo, T-Online carve-outs reacquired at/below IPO price. Implication: spin-offs a mild positive signal, carve-outs from over-levered parents a negative signal (parent raising cash to pay debt).
- Divest mature businesses to fund growth; low app relevance beyond conglomerate analysis.
- Ch17 capital structure: no precise optimal leverage; more to lose than gain. Leverage benefits: interest tax shield; discipline on free cash flow overinvestment (strong cash, low growth firms). Costs: bankruptcy direct costs ~3% of market value; "business erosion" costs much larger (cut R&D/investment, lose customers/employees/suppliers; Chrysler 1979 sales -40%); investor conflicts (underinvestment, asset substitution, covenants).
- Rule: higher leverage fits firms with high, stable returns, low growth, low business risk, fungible/tangible assets (collateral): airlines (planes redeployable; leverage high when operating leases counted), steel, paper, cement. Low leverage fits high growth, volatile earnings, high R&D/advertising, specific assets, durable goods needing long-term service: software, biotech, tech start-ups. Implication for app: set acceptable debt/EBITDA or debt/EV by sector; tangible-asset heavy sectors tolerate more; flag high debt in R&D-heavy/volatile firms.
- Pecking order (internal funds, debt, equity): equity issuance signals overvaluation -> negative signal. Empirical: mature cash-generative firms often highly levered, so theory not conclusive; signaling matters for timing financing. Red flag: equity issuance by a stock apparently cheap.
- Credit ratings: 72% of US/European companies with market cap >$1bn are rated A+ to BBB-; investment-grade threshold BBB-; below it rates jump and market access narrows. Within A+ to BBB- range, value barely depends on capital structure; ROIC and growth matter far more. Implication: leverage matters mainly when it pushes a firm below investment grade; use ROIC/growth as primary drivers.

### p21 (Ch17 capital structure: coverage, ratings, signaling, payout)
- APV value-driver formula: Value = NOPLAT x (1 - g/ROIC)/(ku - g) + PV of tax shields (kD x T x D_t discounted at unlevered cost ku). With stable coverage: tax-shield term uses T/(1-T) x INT / EBITA ratio. Example S&P500 average: ROIC 14%, ku 9%: EV/EBITA barely moves with leverage for coverage > 2; growth 3% vs 6% shifts EV/EBITA much more than leverage. Implication: do not overweight leverage in "cheapness"; ROIC and growth dominate.
- Investment-grade (A+ to BBB-) corresponds to EBITA/interest roughly 5 to 11x. Coverage <2 is unsustainable (business erosion, conflicts, bankruptcy). Zero-debt strong cash generators forgo tax shields (American Home Products ~$1.7bn).
- Effective capital structure references: industry peer leverage, peers with investment-grade ratings, cash flow scenario tests. Industry-specific coverage averages persist.
- Default probabilities within 5 years (S&P, 1981-2003): AAA 0.12%, AA 0.33%, A 0.75%, BBB 3.84%, BB 14.45%, B 33.02%, CCC 61.35% (Moody's: Aaa 0.12, Aa 0.24, A 0.54, Baa 2.16, Ba 11.17, B 31.99, Caa 60.83). B or below is not a wise target. Spreads jump at the BBB- boundary because many investors cannot hold sub-investment grade.
- Ratings mainly explained by size and coverage. All AAA firms have market cap > $50bn. Interest coverage alone explains ~45% of S&P rating variation (R2=0.45).
- Coverage definitions: EBITA/interest, EBITDA/interest, net debt/EBITDA. Net debt/EBITDA better when convertibles or low-coupon debt flatter interest coverage (rollover at higher rates will crush coverage). Market leverage = D/(D+E) (fast-moving: KPN and France Telecom looked fine at boom prices, leverage exploded when equity fell in 2001). Book debt/assets and debt/equity (solvency) poorest predictors for going concerns (Wolters-Kluwer equity/assets <20% yet rated A due to goodwill written off to equity); solvency matters only in distress as collateral proxy. Implication: use interest coverage (EBITA/interest) and net debt/EBITDA, not book D/E; for banks different.
- Leverage and coverage measure the same thing over different horizons; high market leverage with high coverage can indicate single-product firm near end of life cycle (eroding margins).
- Sector adjustment: required coverage for a given rating rises with EBITDA volatility (5-yr std of relative change, top 25 per sector): semiconductor 41%, computer hardware ~30%, steel 26.5%, retail 13.5%, chemicals 11.4%, telecom 10.4%. So steel/semis need much higher coverage than telecom for the same rating. Implication: sector-specific coverage thresholds in app; cyclical sectors need more conservative leverage limits.
- Market-based (Merton) default measures: equity as call option on EV with debt as strike; faster than ratings but blind to what markets miss (KPN 2001 surprised both).
- Worked planning example (EUR 1bn sales, BBB, coverage 5, margin 5%): target BBB+ with coverage >=5 in base and >=2 in downside (margins halved to 2.5%); fund growth by early equity issue (EUR 125m). Teaches downside testing: cut margins 50% and check coverage >= 2.
- Growth consumes cash: growth up -> NPPE and working capital up; capital turnover increases hit cash faster than margin increases. High-growth capex-hungry firms with thin margins need external funding (flag funding gap: FCF after debt repayment and dividends negative).
- Signaling (announcement returns): dividend cut/omission about -9%; seasoned equity offering about -3%; debt issuance about flat; dividend increase about +2% (initiations more); small buyback (<10%) +2-3%; large tender offer buyback (~15% of shares) about +16%. Order of financing preference: debt > equity > dividend cut. Buybacks signal undervaluation and confidence; market reacts to signal, not EPS accretion. Dividend increases follow sustained earnings growth and predict continued earnings strength; but can also signal fewer investment opportunities for growth firms. Implication: dividend growth/buyback yield as quality/confidence signals; equity issuance and dividend cuts as red flags.
- Issuance costs: equity IPO ~17%, SEO ~7% for small issues, fall with size; bonds ~1-2%; scale economies. Microsoft 2004: $75bn return over four years incl. extraordinary dividend. Buybacks 1999 $181bn vs dividends $216bn (NYSE).

### p22 (Ch17 end: buybacks/debt repay/financial engineering; Ch18 Investor Communications)
- Buyback caveat: a big buyback can signal lack of growth opportunities (Merck 2000 $10bn buyback: stock fell 15% in 4 weeks as market inferred R&D pipeline couldn't support 20% EPS growth target). Extraordinary dividends force cash on all holders; repurchases leave choice.
- Debt repayment with excess cash is not a positive signal unless distressed (bond price tracks EV in distress; ABB EUR775m bond buyback in 2004 lifted shares 4%). Overleveraged firms should correct leverage regardless of signal.
- Financial engineering rarely creates value; markets price instruments efficiently. Derivatives useful for large, identifiable, liquid-market risks (swaps, airline fuel hedges only buy time). Scandals: Metallgesellschaft, P&G, Orange County. Off-balance-sheet financing (operating leases, securitization like Ford/GM receivables, project finance) hides debt; investors see through it and IFRS/GAAP now consolidates SPEs/VIEs. Implication: add back operating leases/off-balance debt (lease-adjusted EV and leverage), especially airlines, retailers, auto finance.
- Convertibles: ~8% of new debt issuance 2003 ($145bn); low coupon is not cheap funding (option embedded); not a way to sell equity at a higher price; used by high-growth firms with uncertain credit risk. Implication: treat convertibles as part debt, part equity (dilution) when computing EV and per-share values.
- Summary: capital structure is not a key value driver for firms with reasonable leverage; prevent distress, keep flexibility; ROIC and growth matter more.
- Ch18: management claims of "market undervalues us" usually reflect no real gap or are explained by performance versus peers. Method: segment peers properly (by growth/ROIC/restructuring), then reverse-DCF implied growth. Chemco: EV/EBITA 8.6, ROIC 20.5%, organic growth 2.0% in line with close peers (EV/EBITA 6.8-8.5, ROIC 12-20%, growth 1.9-3.1%) while fast-growth peers had EV/EBITA 9.7-11.2, ROIC 33%, growth 10-12%; restructuring peers showed high multiples (9.3-12.2) because of depressed earnings (ROIC 5-10%). Implied growth of Chemco price = 2%/yr vs fast-growth peers 5-6%. Lesson for app: compare multiples only within true peer groups; exclude peers with temporarily depressed earnings (their P/E and EV/EBITA are inflated); high multiple with low ROIC is a restructuring artifact.
- PharmaCo: mgmt plan 23% growth 3 years then 13% for 10 years gives DCF 60% above market, but price already embeds 12%/yr implied growth vs peers 2-6%. Implication: reverse-DCF implied growth vs peers is a better "expensive/cheap" gauge than P/E alone.
- Aspirational EPS growth: of 1,056 companies, only 16% achieved 10% EPS growth 5 years in a row and only 1% for 10 years. Implication: haircut analyst extrapolation of high EPS growth; mean reversion of growth; EPS growth from buybacks and acquisitions is lower quality.
- Segment disclosure: value concentrated in one unit (BU1 90% of DCF value, 59% of sales but only 20% of press releases) - sum-of-the-parts and segment margins matter; Microsoft resegmented 2003 (Windows/Office >75% margins, Xbox/MSN loss-making).
- Market reacts to earnings surprise depending on whether it has a "tail" (persistent) vs one-time: one-off misses cause much smaller price drops. Implication: normalize one-offs; persistent earnings declines are what to avoid.
- Firms with long histories of success that stop being transparent may be hiding aging products or underinvesting in advertising to keep earnings growing; when they fall they fall hard (red flag: steady earnings with falling ad/R&D spend and low disclosure).
- Earnings guidance ranked 30th of 33 disclosure items by analysts. Sell-side ratings biased: Feb 2005 of 27,000 ratings 40% buy, 52% hold, 8% sell (5:1 buy:sell) - do not rely on analyst recommendations. Hedge funds ~25-30% of turnover (2002) and drive short-term volatility.
- Value vs growth: "growth" = high P/E, but many high P/E stocks are high-ROIC rather than fast-growing; value stocks (low P/E) are mostly mature slow growers or low-ROIC. Investor horizons: fundamental (5y+), news forecasters (12-18 months), event bettors, passive.

### p23 (Ch18 end: investor base; Ch19 multibusiness valuation; Ch20 flexibility start)
- Investor-base analysis (Ch18 end) is IR-oriented; low app relevance. Note: ~70% of company-specific price moves explained by decisions of the 40-100 most active investors; passive/index holders irrelevant to price. Dividend-focused investor base may sell when a mature firm cuts dividend to fund new strategy.
- Ch19 sum-of-the-parts: no consensus on a conglomerate discount; in the authors' experience any discount vs pure-play peers traced to lower growth and/or ROIC in the units ("performance discount, not diversification discount"). Implication: do not mechanically apply a holding/conglomerate discount; check segment ROIC and growth first (relevant to B3 holdings).
- Corporate overhead typically 1-2% of revenue but 10-20% of operating profit, and negative value ~10-20% of enterprise value; keep corporate center as separate cost unit, do not allocate CEO-type costs. Value it with a cash-flow perpetuity (not ROIC formula).
- Segment data: build NOPLAT per unit from segment EBITA, allocate taxes at firm rate, pensions by employees, leases by leased assets; invested capital = segment assets - non-interest-bearing operating liabilities (allocated by revenue) - goodwill (for ROIC ex-goodwill). Reconcile sum of units to consolidated.
- Intercompany eliminations: intersegment profit in inventory; intercompany receivables/payables are equity-like, not working capital (mistreating them understated sub invested capital ~30% and overstated ROIC ~30%).
- Financial subsidiaries (GMAC, GE Capital): value separately on equity cash flows at cost of equity; subtract only industrial (non-financial) debt from EV to avoid double counting. Implication: for industrial groups with finance arms, separate captive finance from EV/EBITDA and net debt.
- Business-unit WACC: use unlevered peer beta relevered at peer-median target D/capital; example soup unlevered beta 0.7, D/C 25%, WACC 7.9%; personal care 8.6%; food service 9.3%. Rf 5%, inflation 2.8%, MRP 4.8%, kd 6%. Corporate beta noisy for diversified firms. Where peers lack, allocate debt to equalize EBITA/interest across units. Legal debt allocation to units is irrelevant.
- Worked example PP Inc.: consolidated EBITA 1,064; NOPLAT 649; invested capital ex-goodwill 2,112; ROIC unit: soup 48%, personal care 30%, food service 21%, total 31%. Sum-of-parts EV/forward EBITA: soup 8.6x (growth 1%, ROIC 48%), personal care 9.6x (growth 3%, ROIC 30%), food service 9.0x (growth 4%, ROIC 21%). Key lesson: high ROIC can outweigh low growth; growth alone does not create value without returns above cost of capital. DCF equity 7,490 vs observed 6,900 (gap ~8%). Corporate costs -873 EV.
- Insight from segment analysis: a secondary unit with ~2x core margin and ROIC can create as much value as the core; top-down view hides it.
- Ch20 flexibility (real options) start: standard DCF ignores managerial flexibility; relevant for single-product, commodity, distress, biotech/internet firms. Example: invest 6,000 for drug paying 500 or 100 perpetuity (50/50), rate 5%: standard NPV 286; deferring until trial result gives contingent NPV 2,143; option value 1,857. Contingent NPV >= standard NPV; flexibility value highest with high uncertainty plus ability to respond; greatest when NPV near zero. Option value drivers: time to expiry, volatility, underlying value, risk-free rate up; investment cost and cash flows lost to competitors down. ROV suits commodity businesses (observable prices: oil, refining, power); decision trees suit technology risk. ROV still needs underlying DCF value.
- Implication for app: option value is a reason some low-earnings biotech/commodity names can carry value beyond DCF, but treat as qualitative; do not add to a cheapness score without data.

### p24 (ch20 Valuing Flexibility end; ch21 Cross-Border start)
- Real options taxonomy: defer (call), abandon (put; liquidation value floors project value), follow-on/compound (R&D, phased capex, exploration), adjust production (expand=call, contract=put, extend/shorten, switch). Options are not additive (expand+abandon 13.5 < 8.4+6.4... sum 14.8).
- Standard NPV undervalues projects with flexibility only when uncertainty is high and decision is a close call (at-the-money); clear go/no-go => option value negligible.
- Two methods: DTA (discount contingent flows at proper rate) vs ROV (replicating portfolio / risk-neutral prob p*=(1+rf-d)/(u-d), discount at rf). Binomial u=e^(sigma*sqrt(T)), d=1/u.
- DTA with project WACC on contingent flows is wrong: option risk is higher (example implied rate 15.5% vs 10% WACC). Lower payoff downside but higher discount rate: leverage-like risk.
- Use DTA when underlying risk is diversifiable (drug trials, geology, consumer acceptance) or inputs are judgmental; use ROV when cash flows tied to traded commodities (mining, oil, power). Discount investment outlays at rf, asset cash flows at WACC.
- Volatility matters: 10y ATM call value 27 at 20% vol vs 35 at 30% vol (+30%). Use long-term vol, not spot vol (oil spot vol misleading).
- R&D example: PV marketed drug 4,314; P(success)=0.15*0.40=6%; standard NPV -169 vs contingent NPV +120 (abandon option). Commercial (beta) risk made no difference; technological risk dominated.
- Implication for app: pre-revenue biotech/exploration names: value = probability-weighted staged option, not plain DCF; screening multiples meaningless there. Flag as "option-like, exclude from cheapness ranking" unless explicit model.
- Cross-border ch21 start: same principles as Part Two; special issues = accounting differences, taxation, translation, cash-flow forecasting in foreign vs domestic currency, cost of capital in foreign currency, FX risk. IFRS adopted widely from 2005 reduces accounting gaps (relevant: B3 uses IFRS, S&P uses US GAAP -> compare with adjustments e.g. R&D, leases, goodwill).

### p25 (ch21 Cross-Border Valuation: accounting, tax, translation, FX)
- IFRS vs US GAAP converged since 2005, but differences matter for cross-listed comparison (B3 = IFRS; S&P = US GAAP): LIFO allowed only US GAAP (inflates COGS, understates inventory; add back LIFO reserve); PP&E revaluation upward allowed IFRS only; interest capitalization allowed US GAAP only; in-process R&D expensed US GAAP vs capitalized IFRS; dev costs capitalizable IFRS (conditions) vs expensed US GAAP; impairment reversal allowed IFRS only; provisions: US GAAP low end of range, IFRS midpoint; JV: proportional or equity method IFRS, equity only US GAAP; IFRS 2005 stock-option expensing.
- Lease classification US GAAP: lease term >=75% of life or PV of payments >=90% of fair value. Fix: capitalize operating leases into invested capital (Ch7) so accounting differences wash out -> app should use lease-adjusted EV/EBITDAR-like or adjusted debt.
- Goodwill: no amortization (2001 US / 2005 IFRS); older history may be amortized or written off against equity (Netherlands) -> for long ROIC history, compute ROIC both with and without goodwill; use EBITA (before goodwill amortization).
- Taxes: use stand-alone tax rate on operating income; fiscal grouping, tax credits, treaties; US foreign tax credit can raise effective rate on low-tax foreign subs. Dividend imputation effect usually small (UK example 11% credit) -> no adjustment needed.
- Translation: analyze subsidiaries in local currency; consolidated translation gains/losses must be removed from cash flow. Current method (moderate inflation); US GAAP temporal vs IFRS inflation-adjusted for hyperinflation (>=~100% cumulative 3y). Net income and equity differ significantly across methods (example NI 32 / 95 / 48) -> Brazil/Argentina/Turkey reported EPS and equity are unreliable in high-inflation periods.
- Consistency rule: cash flow currency = discount rate currency; same inflation in forecasts and in risk-free rate. Valuing foreign-currency flows at domestic WACC is a common mistake. Value must be same in any currency: spot-rate method (discount in foreign, convert at spot) equals forward-rate method (convert at forward, discount in domestic). Forward via interest rate parity: Xt = X0 * ((1+rF)/(1+rD))^t.
- Example inconsistency: sales forecast inflation 10% but cost of capital built on long-term rates implying 5% -> DCF too high. Relevant for BRL (Selic/NTN-B inflation vs IPCA assumptions).
- Cost of capital: use global investor perspective: global market risk premium and global beta (not local index beta); local indexes concentrated (top 10 firms 70-90% in small markets; industry composition explains ~49% of European index returns). S&P 500 proxy for global premium. Realized 1900-1999 equity premium vs bills 2-7% across countries (US 5.8).
- No ad hoc discount-rate add-ons for political/country/currency risk: handle country risk in cash-flow scenarios; currency risk is in spot/forward rates and is diversifiable (real FX volatility: Argentina 28%, Brazil 12.7%, regional portfolio 4.5%).
- App implication: for B3 screening do not simply add big Brazil country risk premium to WACC; compare B3 and S&P on same scale by converting inflation-consistent real or same-currency terms; flag companies with large USD debt vs BRL revenue.

### p26 (ch21 end currency risk; ch22 Emerging Markets: inflation, scenarios)
- Currency risk: no premium in WACC; matters only if it threatens investment programs/solvency. Economic exposure >> accounting exposure. Competitive exposure (revenue vs cost currency mismatch, e.g. Heineken USD sales/EUR cost) is the one that matters; transaction/portfolio exposure minor. Check debt currency matches cash-flow currency. App flag: USD debt vs BRL revenue with weak coverage = distress risk (not a discount-rate add-on).
- EM valuation is harder: macro uncertainty, illiquid markets, capital controls, weaker accounting, political risk. Recommend triangulation: (1) scenario DCF with probability-weighted cash flows, (2) DCF with country risk premium, (3) trading/transaction multiples.
- PPP holds in the long run (Brazil 1964-2004: nominal currency worthless, PPP-adjusted real value ~stable, ~$90m of $100m). Short run deviations 20-30% for years. Assume convergence to PPP; sustainable margin/ROIC should not depend on temporary FX misvaluation. App: normalize margins of exporters (Vale, Suzano, Petrobras) for BRL level; don't extrapolate peak-FX margins.
- High inflation distorts statements: growth overstated (deflate by inflation index), capital turnover overstated (PPE at historical cost), operating margin overstated (low depreciation, inventory holding gains), solvency ratios (D/E, debt/assets) unreliable. Use EBITDA/sales vs peers and EBITDA/interest coverage instead. Extreme inflation >25%/yr: companies report year-end currency.
- Brazil tax example: inflation accounting abolished 1996, tax rate ~33% (30.5% + social contribution), interest on equity (JCP) deductible with 15% withholding -> payout/tax-shield effect to account for in NOPLAT and dividend yield on B3.
- Project in both real and nominal; must give identical value. WACC link: (1+WACC_N)=(1+WACC_R)(1+inflation). Real-terms ROIC overstated with positive NWC (monetary loss on NWC); nominal ROIC rises over time as nominal PPE lags revenue (ConsuCo 8.4%->10.1% nominal vs 7.6%->6.6% real). Need longer explicit horizon under high inflation.
- ConsuCo (Brazilian consumer goods) case: ROIC ex goodwill 6-8% real, WACC higher => marginal value creation; EBITDA/sales rose 5.7%->9.2%; sales/capacity stable.
- Scenario DCF vs country risk premium: example identical plants, EM plant 25% prob of crisis with cash flow -55% => value 1,917 vs 2,222 (86%); equivalent CRP only 0.7% (8.2% vs 7.5%). Never apply CRP to expected (probability-weighted) cash flows = double counting. A 3% CRP implies ~70% distress probability -> managers underestimate CRP impact.
- Arguments against CRP: country risks largely diversifiable (EM portfolio ROIC much less volatile than single countries); risks unequal across companies (banks hit, exporters benefit from devaluation); no systematic way to compute it; sovereign spread is promised not expected yield. After 1-2 years sales/profits in stable currency regained trend after crises.
- App implication: for B3, do not mechanically stack an EM premium; instead stress-test: haircut earnings in a crisis scenario with probability, rank by resilience (exporters hedge vs importers, low USD debt).

### p27 (ch22 EM cost of capital/results; ch23 High-Growth start)
- Crisis probabilities: 20 EM economies/20 yrs had real GDP decline >5% about once per 5 years (=20% downside prob); ConsuCo downside prob 25-35%; downside = 4-5 yrs negative cash margins (-10 to -5%), real sales -10 to -15%/yr, DCF value ~half of base. Result: base 253/sh, downside 67/sh -> weighted 188-206/sh.
- EM cost of capital = global cost of capital adjusted for local inflation and capital structure. CAPM with global beta, global MRP 4.5-5.5%, no country premium when discounting expected (probability-weighted) flows. Add CRP only if discounting business-as-usual flows.
- Local nominal risk-free = (1+US rf)*(1+local infl)/(1+US infl)-1 (Brazil 2004: 4.6% US rf, infl 7.1%->4.4%; Brazil rf 9.8%->7.0%). Do not use dollar sovereign bonds or low-rated local government yield as rf blindly. Cost of equity ConsuCo 14.0% falling to 11.1% (beta 0.8, MRP 5%). Unlevered industry beta 0.55; relever at D/EV 0.3.
- Cost of debt = local rf + systematic credit spread (not full country spread) ; ConsuCo B+ => 11.5% falling to 8.8%; after-tax 5.8%; tax 34% (25% IRPJ + 9% CSLL). Use only taxes applicable to interest for the tax shield. Promised yield > expected yield. Multinationals (Coca-Cola, Colgate) have no higher debt cost despite EM exposure.
- WACC base 12.0% -> 9.4% nominal (4.5-4.7% real). Downside scenario WACC explodes during hyperinflation years (52%, 146%) -> year-by-year WACC.
- Capital structure in EM unusually conservative; converge to global; use APV if explicit path.
- CRP guidance: don't use raw sovereign spread (only fits if cash flows move with gov bond); estimates vary hugely and are offset by aggressive growth/ROIC (Brazil chemicals: 11% CRP advisor assumed 10% real growth and 46% ROIC to land at same 4.5x EBITDA). Brazil CRP implied by market ~0.5%, not 5%: top 30 Brazilian cos median EV/EBITA 7.4 (Oct 2004) with ROIC 11%, infl 4.4%, real growth 3% => implied WACC 10.3% vs CAPM 9.8%. Brazilian stocks real return only 3.8%/yr over 10y, cannot support 12% real required return with 5% CRP.
- Multiples triangulation: ConsuCo implied ~7x EV/EBITDA 2005 vs peers avg 8.3 (median 7.1); Brazil domicile does not matter much for relative pricing. Valuation range 175-205 R$/share vs 12m price 150-270 -> use ranges not point. EM prices noisy: low free float, controlling owners, illiquidity (days to trade float ~130 vs ~100), volatility.
- App implication: B3 reference multiples ~7x EBITDA / ~7.4x EBITA for large caps in 2004 era (historical, recalibrate); discount rate for B3 = global-based + inflation differential, not 5% CRP. Use ranges and scenario haircuts.
- Ch23 High-growth (>15% organic growth): classic scenario-weighted DCF beats P/E (earnings volatile, negative) and real options (needs same inputs). Losses coexist with rising value because growth investment (customer acquisition, R&D) is expensed; capitalize hidden investments to see true ROIC.
- Process: start from the future (10-15 yr steady state: market size, share, margin, capital turnover) and work backward; multiple scenarios with probabilities consistent with historical corporate growth evidence. Amazon example: ROIC = margin*(turns)*(1-t): 8% x 8x x (1-.375) ~40% long-term; peer retailers EBITA margins 5-9%, turns 1.2-11, ROIC 6.5-63%. Scenario equity values 21.2/13.5/7.9/2.9 $bn.
- Margin ramp: improvement per year decays (11pp -> 4.4 -> 2.2 ...), model convergence not linear.
- Red flag: forecasts of share gain/ROIC far above peers; competitive entry erodes ROIC; the highest-ROIC peer (Dell 63%) not a valid anchor.

### p28 (ch23 High-Growth end; ch24 Cyclicals; ch25 Financial Institutions start)
- High-growth: scenario weights must be calibrated to historical growth of past winners (Amazon after $4bn revenue grew like Home Depot/Microsoft/Wal-Mart, so >$30bn by 2009 inconsistent with evidence). Weighted Amazon equity 12.9bn (20%/50%/20%/10% on 21.2/13.5/7.9/2.9) = $31.9/share vs market ~18bn. Value drop A->B (growth) -36%; B->C (margin 8%->5.2%) -42%: margin sensitivity bigger than growth.
- 1999 edition scenarios (rev up to $85bn, margins 14%) were far too bullish; only the worst case materialized; buyer after 2000 lost ~7%/yr. Lesson: peak-market prices require unrealistic assumptions; range of scenarios tightens as company matures (monthly vol 40% -> 12%). Even if the scenario is right, the return may be only ~13%/yr because success is priced in; picking winners is hard, diversify.
- In pessimistic scenarios, check debt value (default risk) before computing equity.
- Cyclicals (ch24): DCF value of a cycle company should be much less volatile than earnings (high/low years cancel; only normalized trend matters). Yet prices are volatile. Analyst consensus ignores cycles (36 US cyclicals 1985-97: forecasts always upward-sloping, even at peak) -> consensus P/E and forward EPS at peak are misleading. Market behaves like ~50% perfect-foresight / 50% zero-foresight blend (cycle may break to a new trend).
- Method: value (1) normal-cycle scenario with continuing value on normalized (mid-cycle) profits/ROIC, never peak or trough; (2) new-trend scenario from recent performance; assign probabilities with economic rationale (demand, entry/exit, technology). Cyclicality matters mainly for solvency.
- Cyclicality is driven by supply: companies over-invest at high prices (commodity chemicals 1980-2001: investment peaks when ROIC peaks, then ROIC falls ~2 yrs later). Capex at peak = warning; optimally timed asset purchases would lift IRR 4%->34%. Contrarian capex is rare.
- App implications cyclicals (steel, mining, pulp, oil, chemicals, airlines): do NOT rank by trailing/forward P/E (lowest at peak, highest at trough); use mid-cycle/normalized EBITDA or EBIT (5-10y average margin x current revenue) and EV/normalized EBITA; flag rising capex/PPE growth with record ROIC as peak signal; flag net debt/normalized EBITDA.
- Financial institutions (ch25): use equity cash flow (not enterprise DCF): ECF = net income - increase in equity + other comprehensive income (= dividends + buybacks - issuance). Discount at cost of equity. Highly levered, outsiders lack info (credit losses, ALM mismatch).
- Risk capital: outside-in assume risk capital = book equity. Basel/RWA, Tier 1: capital tied to risk. Key drivers = growth and ROE (not ROIC). Forecast equity as % of assets (or RWA) so leverage consistent; don't forecast dividends independently.
- Goodwill: compute ROE with and without goodwill (insurer example 7% incl. vs 32% excl.); ROE ex-goodwill indicates incremental ROE for cash flow; ROE incl. goodwill shows premium paid. Expense stock options.
- Bank CV: CV = NI*(1 - g/RONE)/(ke - g), NI before goodwill amortization, RONE ~ ROE before goodwill. Economic profit = Equity*(ROE - ke) = NI - Equity*ke. Value = starting equity + PV(economic profit). Do not relever betas for banks. McKinsey: ALM mismatch more than leverage drives bank return volatility.
- Bank economics: NII = true customer spread (value-creating) + maturity-mismatch income (unsustainable, not value-creating once risk-adjusted). Fee income stable and financing independent; trading/securities gains volatile. Costs: loan loss provisions (hard for outsiders) and noninterest expense; efficiency ratio = noninterest expense / revenue (Big Bank 58% -> 60%, rising = warning). Spread model example: loan spread 4%, deposit spread 3%, cash opportunity cost.
- Big Bank: ROE 19% (28% ex goodwill) vs ke 8.4% -> value creating. App: for banks (Itau, Bradesco, BB, JPM) use P/B vs ROE-ke, P/E, not EV-based; Gordon-type P/B = (ROE-g)/(ke-g).

### p29 (ch25 Big Bank + insurer Acme; Appendices A-D start)
- Big Bank outside-in model: forecast interest on loans/deposits via margins, loan loss provision as % loans, noninterest expense % revenue, tax % pretax; equity set by RWA x Tier1 ratio (8% min) + goodwill; dividends/buybacks plug. Capital gains forecast zero. Output: ROE incl goodwill 17-18%, ex goodwill ~24%, growth 3.4% (< nominal GDP 4.5-5%), payout ~86% of NI. ke 8.4%, CV growth 3.5%, RONE 25% -> equity value 61.6bn; fwd P/E 17.1, P/B 3.0 (above peers because spread of ROE over ke is large). Sanity-check: value = book equity + PV economic profit.
- Forecast ratios to monitor for banks: loans/deposits, loans/assets, RWA/loans (134%), equity/assets 8.5-9%, ROA ~1.5%, efficiency ratio, noninterest income/revenue ~45%, provision/loans.
- Inside view: split into retail/wholesale/treasury with transfer prices; RAROC = net profit / economic capital by unit; excess capital should be paid out in year 1.
- Insurers: revenue = premiums + investment income (spread business) + fees + realized gains; costs = benefits & claims (loss ratio in P&C), DAC amortization (real cost), commissions, reinsurance netted. Combined ratio = total costs/premiums, often >100% with returns coming from investing float. Reserve increases are negative signal (prior under-reserving). GAAP vs statutory: DAC capitalized under GAAP, expensed under statutory; statutory conservative, surplus differs from GAAP equity. Separate-account assets/liabilities offset - exclude.
- Insurer ECF must include unrealized gains (OCI): economic cash flow even if unrealized. Acme: ROE 10.5-11.5% vs ke 10.5% -> no value creation; DCF 14.7bn within 10% of market; fwd P/E 12.8; P/B 1.3. Value depends on investment success. Life insurer: embedded value = PV in-force cash flows + book equity (European practice).
- General: if ROE ~ ke, P/B ~1; premium to book requires sustainable ROE>ke. 
- Appendix A: Value = IC0 * (ROIC-g)/(WACC-g) = IC0 + EP1/(WACC-g), where EP = IC*(ROIC-WACC). Key value driver formula V = NOPLAT1*(1-g/ROIC)/(WACC-g). Requires WACC>g and ROIC>g. If economic profit expected <0, EV trades below invested capital (observed in practice); if EP=0, EV = book IC. Growth adds value only if ROIC>WACC (ROIC=WACC => growth irrelevant).
- Appendix B: Discounted EP = discounted FCF; EP must use beginning-of-year invested capital. FCF = NOPLAT - increase in IC; EP = NOPLAT - WACC*IC(t-1). Positive-EP companies trade at premium to book invested capital.
- Appendix C: WACC uses market-value weights (not book); constant WACC only if leverage constant. APV: V = FCF/ku + PV(tax shield) (tax shield discount rate debatable; if at kd then WACC must change).
- Appendix D start: unlevered cost of equity/ beta unlever-relever (continues p30).

### p30 (Appendix D levering; Appendix E leverage and P/E; Index)
- App D: MM: Vu + Vtxa = D + E; if debt is constant % of EV (tax shield risk = operating risk) ku = D/(D+E)*kd + E/(D+E)*ke, and ke = ku + (D/E)(ku - kd). If tax shield risk = debt risk: ke = ku + ((D - Vtxa)/E)(ku - kd). Beta: bL = bU + (bU - bD)*D/E; if debt riskless bL = bU*(1+D/E). Error small for investment-grade, large for highly levered or junk debt -> estimate debt beta then. Use market values of D and E.
- App E: P/E depends on unlevered P/E, kd and leverage: levered P/E = 1/kd + [-(1/kd)... ] i.e. P/E rises with leverage if unlevered P/E > 1/kd, falls if < 1/kd (no taxes, no distress). Therefore P/E is not comparable across firms with different leverage; this is a core reason EV/EBITA beats P/E. Example: kd 6% => 1/kd = 16.7; low-P/E companies get an even lower P/E with leverage and look "cheaper" for the wrong reason.
- Index: pointers only (multiples ch12, cost of capital ch10, performance mgmt etc.), no new content.

## Book synthesis

### (a) Value drivers and key formulas
- Core thesis: value = capital invested at ROIC above WACC, times how long and how much you can reinvest at that spread. Three drivers: ROIC (incl. RONIC on new capital), growth g, WACC. Growth adds value only if RONIC > WACC; neutral at RONIC = WACC; destroys value below.
- NOPLAT = EBITA - operating cash taxes (taxes on EBITA, not reported; exclude nonoperating income and interest; add back lease interest). Use marginal rate for tax shields, operating/cash rate for NOPLAT.
- Invested capital (IC) = operating working capital (working cash ~2% of sales only; excess cash excluded) + net PP&E + capitalized operating leases + other net operating assets (+ goodwill/acquired intangibles shown separately; add back cumulative amortization/write-offs). = debt & equivalents + equity & equivalents - nonoperating assets. Equivalents: unfunded pension/OPEB, leases, restructuring/decommissioning reserves (debt); deferred taxes, income-smoothing provisions (equity).
- ROIC = NOPLAT / average IC = (1-cash tax) x EBITA/Revenue x Revenue/IC. Always report ex-goodwill (operating quality) AND incl.-goodwill (what acquisitions cost; P&G 41% vs 21%).
- FCF = NOPLAT + depreciation - gross investment = NOPLAT - net investment = NOPLAT x (1 - IR); g = RONIC x IR.
- Value driver (Zen) formula: EV = NOPLAT1 x (1 - g/RONIC) / (WACC - g). Needs WACC > g, ROIC > g.
- Economic profit EP = IC x (ROIC - WACC) = NOPLAT - WACC x IC(beginning). EV = IC0 + PV(EP); EP1/(WACC-g) for growing perpetuity. EP = 0 => EV = IC; EP < 0 => EV below IC. DCF = EP model exactly (App B).
- Multiples derived: EV/NOPLAT = (1 - g/RONIC)/(WACC - g); EV/EBITA = (1-T)(1 - g/ROIC)/(WACC - g); EV/IC = ROIC(1 - g/RONIC)/(WACC - g). At ROIC = WACC, EV/EBITA = (1-T)/WACC irrespective of growth (30% tax, 9% WACC = 7.8x). Equity analog: P/B = (ROE - g)/(ke - g); banks: CV = NI(1 - g/RONE)/(ke - g); EP = Equity x (ROE - ke).
- WACC = kd(1-Tm) D/V + ke E/V with target MARKET weights; ke = rf(10y local govt) + beta x MRP (global, 4.5-5.5%); beta = unlevered industry median relevered; kd = long-term YTM/rating-based (not coupon; for BB and below use BBB+~0.5%). ke = ku + (D/E)(ku - kd); bL = bU(1 + D/E) if debt riskless. Leverage is not a free lunch.
- Equity bridge: operations + excess cash + nonconsolidated stakes + other nonoperating = EV; minus debt, leases, unfunded pensions (after tax), preferred, contingent liabilities, option value/converts, minority interest = equity. Use undiluted shares when claims deducted at option value.
- Empirical anchors (US 1963-2003): median ROIC ex-goodwill 9% (IQR 5-15%); percentile: <5% 25%, <10% 56%, <15% 74%, <20% 84%, <30% 92%; ROIC>20% ~ top 1 in 6. Sector medians: pharma 18, household 15, software 15, media 14.7, semis 12, food-bev 11, retailing 9, materials 8.4, energy 7.7, transport 6.9, telecom 6.5, utilities 6.2. ROIC persistent (50% of >20% stay >20% after 10y; <5% group 43% stay), growth is not (>20% growers ~8% in 5y, 5% in 10y; median real growth 6.3%, 1/4 shrink). Market value/IC explained 46% by ROIC + growth; growth only priced when ROIC > ~9-12%.
- Returns come from CHANGES in expectations, not level of performance: good company at full price = ordinary return. 5y dividends = ~9% of price; ~60-80% of value is in continuing value.

### (b) Using multiples correctly
- Hierarchy: DCF first; multiples are a check, require as much work as a forecast. Never an industry-average shortcut.
- Four best practices: (1) peers with similar ROIC and growth, (2) forward not trailing, (3) EV/EBITA not P/E, (4) adjust EV and EBITA for nonoperating items.
- Which multiple: EV/EBITA primary (after D&A, so capex burden captured; independent of capital structure; excludes nonoperating items). EV/EBITDA only within same asset intensity (own-vs-outsource example: 3.0x vs 6.0x for identical EV/EBITA 7.5x). P/E only for financials or similar-leverage peers. Price/Sales weak (only for negative earnings, with similar margins). PEG flawed (zero-intercept, ignores ROIC): regress multiple on growth and ROIC instead. P/B or EV/IC must be read with ROIC (justified EV/IC = ROIC(1-g/RONIC)/(WACC-g)).
- Why EV/EBITA beats P/E: P/E depends on leverage (levered P/E rises with debt if unlevered P/E > 1/kd, falls if below; crossover 1/kd, e.g. 20x at kd 5%); includes nonoperating gains/losses and one-offs (AOL Time Warner P/E meaningless after $98bn write-off); cash inflates P/E (cash earns ~2% after tax = P/E ~50; Circuit City 22.3x P/E vs 15.1x ex-cash vs cheapest on EV/EBITDA); amortization/goodwill/accounting noise; EPS can be bought via buybacks or leveraged acquisitions.
- Adjustments: EV = equity + debt + capitalized leases + after-tax unfunded pensions + option value + minorities + preferred - excess cash - nonoperating assets; EBITA = reported + implied lease interest (+/- pension interest/return, - stock option expense); strip one-offs. HD: raw 19% apparent discount vs hardline sector shrank to 5% after using right peer (Lowe's), forward, EV/EBITA, lease and cash adjustment.
- Forward vs trailing: forward E/P std dev 3.7% vs 6.0% historical; median pricing error 23% (hist) -> 18% (1y fwd) -> 16% (2y fwd). Use forward (consensus, haircut for upward analyst bias) or normalized trailing; at cycle peaks consensus ignores cycles.
- Peer choice: GICS-like industry groups beat SIC; segment multi-business firms into sum-of-parts; exclude peers with depressed/negative earnings (inflated multiples; restructuring peers 9-12x on ROIC 5-10%); use median or harmonic mean (average of earnings yields), not arithmetic mean; compare like ROIC/growth and same currency/inflation regime; don't mix cap-weighted index P/E (30 in 1999) with median stock (23).
- Reading a gap: explain it via growth, ROIC, WACC, tax (the four drivers) before calling it mispricing; reverse DCF to get market-implied growth and compare to peers/saturation (HD 1998 price implied 26% growth). Multiples are regime dependent: fair market P/E ~15 normal, ~10 in high inflation/rates.
- Exit multiples in DCF: never reuse today's P/E; right multiple depends on future g, RONIC, WACC.

### (c) Emerging markets, inflation, cyclicals, banks
- Emerging markets/Brazil: same DCF, plus (i) consistent monetary assumptions (cash-flow inflation = inflation in rf; value same in any currency; spot method = forward method via interest-rate parity); (ii) analyze in real and nominal; PPP holds long-run, normalize FX-inflated margins; (iii) inflation distorts growth, turns, margins, solvency ratios: use EBITDA/sales, coverage EBITDA/interest, deflated growth; nominal ROIC inflated as old assets sit at cost; (iv) risk via scenario DCF (crisis probability ~20-35%, cash flows ~-50%) discounted at global-based WACC, NOT a large country premium; CRP only on business-as-usual flows, and Brazil's implied CRP was ~0.5% not 5% (median EV/EBITA 7.4x for top 30 in 2004); (v) global beta and MRP 4.5-5.5%; local rf = (1+US rf)(1+local infl)/(1+US infl)-1; kd = rf + systematic spread only; (vi) triangulate with multiples and use a value range (+/-15-30%); (vii) price noise: low free float, controlling owners, illiquidity.
- Inflation/IFRS: LIFO vs FIFO, hyperinflation translation differences (temporal vs inflation-adjusted), >25% inflation = restated statements; lease capitalization neutralizes lessee/owner gap; JCP and tax credits in Brazil need a normalized operating tax rate.
- Cyclicals: do not use peak or trough earnings; normalize NOPLAT at mid-cycle in CV; two-scenario valuation (normal cycle vs new trend), probability weighted; consensus forecasts miss cycles (upward-sloping at peak); market behaves ~50/50; supply (capex herding at peak ROIC) causes cycles; cyclicality mostly matters for solvency (coverage >=2 in downturn; need higher coverage for volatile EBITDA: semis 41% vol, steel 26%, telecom 10%).
- Banks/insurers: equity cash flow = NI - increase in equity + OCI; discount at ke; drivers are growth and ROE (not ROIC); equity set by RWA x Tier 1 (8% min); ROE with and without goodwill; economic profit = Equity x (ROE - ke); value ~ book + PV(EP); P/B = (ROE-g)/(ke-g); do not relever beta; NII = true spread + mismatch income (not value-creating); fee income stable, trading/gains volatile; loan loss provisions and efficiency ratio (rising = warning); insurers: combined ratio, loss ratio, DAC, reserve increases are negative surprises, include unrealized gains (OCI), ROE ~ ke => P/B ~ 1.
- Other: high-growth/pre-profit firms need scenario DCF starting from the future steady state; real options only for commodity/R&D optionality; conglomerate discount is usually a performance (ROIC/growth) discount, overhead = negative 10-20% of EV.

### (d) Red flags and value traps
- Low multiple with ROIC <= WACC (deserved); low P/E from high leverage or peak cyclical earnings; low EV/EBITDA on capital-hungry or lease-heavy business; cash-inflated P/E mistakenly read as expensive and vice versa.
- Growth with RONIC < WACC (acquisitions at premium ~30%, serial acquirers, goodwill build-up; ROIC incl. goodwill far below ex-goodwill; cost synergies credible, revenue synergies unreliable).
- EPS growth from buybacks (buy P/E < 1/[kd(1-t)]), debt-financed acquisitions, asset-sale gains, tax/one-offs; short-termism (55% of execs would sacrifice value to hit EPS); margin gains with falling R&D/advertising/capex; price-only gains with falling market share.
- Dividends funded by debt (payout + reinvestment > 100% of cash flow); dividend cut/omission, equity issuance by a "cheap" stock, large buyback signaling no growth; falling revenue per store with rising store count.
- Hidden debt: operating leases, unfunded pensions/OPEB (e.g., autos, airlines, state-company funds), contingencies/litigation provisions, off-balance-sheet entities, preferred, minority interests, convertible/option dilution; Enron-type unconsolidated subs; income-smoothing provisions ("other provisions" growing/reversing); low cash tax rate from rising deferrals that may reverse; pension income inflating EBITA.
- Leverage: coverage EBITA/interest < 2 unsustainable; investment grade ~5-11x; falling below BBB- is the breakpoint; B or below not wise (5y default 33%); thresholds sector-specific.
- Weak firms stay weak: ROIC <5% 43% still <5% after 10y; old shrinking retailers/industries with ROIC < WACC; steady margins in aging franchises that stop disclosing; patent cliffs (50-75% drop).
- Analyst optimism (5:1 buy:sell; growth forecasts upward biased, only 16% of firms achieve 10% EPS growth 5y in a row); consensus ignores cycles; impairments are lagging signals (stock already -35%).
- Valuation red flags: CV assumptions (g > economy, RONIC > WACC forever, ROIC >> peers), NOPLAT/(WACC-g) with no reinvestment, double-counting country risk, discounting domestic-currency WACC on foreign flows, using local-index beta, high-priced "priced for perfection" stocks (expectation treadmill), apparent mispricing in holding/dual-class/carve-out structures (can persist; need catalyst).
- Distressed/binary names: equity is an option; debt valued below face; screen "cheapness" is unreliable.
- Emerging-market accounting: inflation-flattered ROIC and margins, FX-driven revenue for exporters, hyperinflation restatement, capital hoarding.

### (e) Top 10 implications for the stock-screening app (B3 + S&P 500)
1. Core score = justified vs actual multiple: compute justified EV/EBITA = (1-T)(1-g/ROIC)/(WACC-g) from sector-fading growth and ROIC, compare with actual (adjusted) EV/EBITA; add reverse-DCF market-implied growth and flag if implied growth exceeds peers/GDP or saturation.
2. Always pair any cheapness indicator with ROIC-WACC spread (and EP/market cap for size); a low multiple with ROIC <= WACC is not cheap; growth credited only when ROIC > WACC.
3. Primary multiple: EV/EBITA, forward (consensus, haircut) with normalized trailing fallback; EV adjusted for leases, after-tax unfunded pensions, minorities, preferred, option dilution, minus excess cash (working cash 2% of sales) and nonoperating stakes; EBITA adds back lease interest and deducts SBC; strip one-offs. P/E only secondary and only vs similarly levered peers; banks: P/B vs ROE-ke and P/E.
4. Peer groups: GICS-like, sector-relative percentiles using median/harmonic mean of earnings yields; exclude negative/depressed-earnings peers; compare vs own 5-10y history; use sector ROIC medians (pharma 18, utilities 6, etc.) and sector leverage norms (utilities D/V 47% fine, software 0%); treat differences within ~15% as noise.
5. Quality overlay: ROIC level and persistence (3-5y), with and without goodwill, organic/constant-currency growth, cash conversion, reinvestment ratio, coverage EBITA/interest (<2 red; sector-adjusted), net debt/EBITDA; ROIC>20% needs a moat check; penalize ROE inflated by leverage (ROE = ROIC + D/E(ROIC - after-tax kd)).
6. Cyclicals (materials, energy, steel, pulp, airlines, chemicals): value on mid-cycle/normalized EBITA (5-10y avg margin) not current P/E; flag peak signals (record ROIC with accelerating capex/PPE growth, consensus upward-sloping EPS); cap leverage by EBITDA volatility.
7. Growth treatment: fade growth quickly (>20% growers ~8% in 5y), cap terminal g at nominal GDP/rf, RONIC = WACC default except moat sectors (brands, patents: staples, pharma); do not credit acquisitive growth; haircut analyst EPS growth (upward bias) and EPS growth from buybacks.
8. Brazil/EM handling: BRL nominal WACC from local rf with consistent inflation (NTN-B vs prefixado breakeven), global beta/MRP, no stacked big country premium; instead scenario haircut for crisis (20-35% probability, large drawdown) and resilience flags (USD debt vs BRL revenue, exporters' FX-inflated margins normalized to PPP, inflation-distorted ROIC/turns, JCP/tax credits); use value ranges not points; adjust IFRS vs US GAAP (LIFO reserve, R&D capitalization, impairment reversal) before cross-market comparison.
9. Value-trap filter: hidden debt (leases, pensions, litigation provisions, off-balance), dividends > FCF or debt-funded, equity issuance, serial acquirers with goodwill, falling R&D/capex/ad spend with margin gains, shrinking revenue per store, price-only growth with falling share, income-smoothing provisions, low cash tax from deferrals, weak-ROIC persistence (<5% for years), holding/dual-class discounts without catalyst.
10. Present uncertainty: sensitivity grid (WACC +-0.5pt ~ +-10-17% value, g +1pt, margin +1pt), scenario values with probabilities, margin of safety >= 15-20%, remember returns follow changes in expectations (cheap alone is not enough; combine with revisions/quality), use median-based universe benchmarks (not cap-weighted), and treat distressed/pre-profit/option-like names (biotech, exploration) as a separate non-ranked class.
